# The mountain year: seasonal herding in the Himachal hills, 1881 to 1961

Earlier versions: [candidate-topics.md](candidate-topics.md) (16 topics),
[shimla-hill-states-sutlej.md](shimla-hill-states-sutlej.md) (Shimla and the Sutlej),
[begar-land-caste-12-villages.md](begar-land-caste-12-villages.md) and [begar-land-caste-5-villages.md](begar-land-caste-5-villages.md) (begar, land and
caste), [joint-household-himachal.md](joint-household-himachal.md) (the joint household). The two-valley
comparison that grew out of this file is
[two-valleys-sutlej-ravi.md](two-valleys-sutlej-ravi.md).

The interpretation works in two steps. The Annales step reads the geography
and the time scales and makes the comparisons. The Marxist step then looks
inside each medium-term period (conjuncture) for the contradictions that drove
change. The interpretive framework section sets this out.

The sections follow the three stages in `project.docx`. Every quote was
checked against the extracted text in `text/`, with obvious OCR errors
corrected. Section 7 gives the PDF file and page of each key quote. Pages are
PDF page numbers, the counter a PDF viewer shows. `scripts/pageref.py` finds
the page of any other quote.

### Terms used

- **Transhumance.** The seasonal movement of herds between high summer
  pastures and low winter grazing, by people who keep a fixed home and fields.
  Nomads, by contrast, have no fixed home.
- **Gaddis.** A herding and farming people whose home is Brahmaur in Chamba.
  They cross the Dhauladhar range to Kangra each winter.
- **Dhar, odhwari.** High summer pastures.
- **Malundi, pohal, fawal, phogala.** Shepherds. A malundi tends his own flock.
  A pohal, fawal or phogala is hired.
- **Permit.** A Forest Department licence stating how many animals a shepherd
  may graze. Grazing fees are paid per head.
- **Longue durée, conjuncture, event.** Braudel's three time scales. The longue
  durée is geographical time, which changes over centuries: mountains, rivers,
  snow. A conjuncture is a period of a few decades with its own economic and
  political arrangement, such as princely rule over the pastures. An event is a
  single dated change, such as the 1948 merger.
- **Dialectic, contradiction.** In the Marxist reading, change comes from the
  conflict between two opposed forces inside one arrangement, for example
  between the shepherds who use a pasture and the ruler who owns it. The
  conflict produces a new arrangement, which carries its own contradictions.

## Stage 1. Research question

### The problem in the sources

Every source layer describes the same annual cycle in similar terms.

- **1881, Barnes, quoted by Ibbetson**: the Gaddis' wealth "consists of flocks
  of sheep and goats, which they feed half the year (the winter months) in the
  valleys of Kangra, and for the other half drive across the range into the
  territories of Chamba. ... Many Gadis cultivate the winter crops or wheat in
  Kangra, and returning with their flocks grow the summer or rain crop at
  'Barmor'."
- **1910, Rose**: "the summers being spent in the higher mountains of Pangi and
  Lahul; and the winters in the low hills bordering on the plains ... The
  yearly exodus to Kangra takes place in October and November, and the return
  journey in April and May."
- **1931 Census Report**: "While the plain dwellers go to the hills in summer,
  the hillmen of the lofty ranges leave their homes with their sheep and
  cattle to spend the winter in the valleys and plains of the neighbouring
  districts."
- **1961, Brahmaur**: "In the beginning of the winter (October and November),
  the flocks are driven to the low hills of Kangra, Nurpur, Pathankot and
  Jammu." "Out of 99 local households, as many as 61 move out in winter
  months."
- **1961, Kanum (Kinnaur)**: "During winter people migrate to lower hills
  alongwith their flocks laden with chilgozas, wool, woollen patties."

The months and routes in 1961 are the same as in 1881 and 1910. Control over
the pastures changed in that time.

- **Before 1948**: "In Chamba all grazing rights belonged to the rajah of
  Chamba." The right to collect grazing fees "was auctioned by the rajah of
  Chamba to the highest bidder, who was usually a Gaddi." The 1950 rate was 2
  annas 6 pies per goat and 1 anna 6 pies per sheep per grazing district
  (Newell).
- **After 1948**: "These rights to grazing tax have now been taken over by the
  ... State Forest Department." A receipt "is however not issued unless the
  shepherd has a permit." Newell adds that "these permits are freely bought
  and sold amongst shepherds so that shepherds have now become closed
  corporations."

Ownership of the flocks is unequal. In Brahmaur (1961 survey):

| Caste | Households | Households with flocks | Sheep and goats | Per owning household |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Brahman | 34 | 20 | 2,027 | 101 |
| Rajput | 49 | 20 | 2,364 | 118 |
| Sipi | 15 | 8 | 93 | 12 |
| Rehara | 1 | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| **Total** | **99** | **48** | **4,484** | **93** |

Brahmans and Rajputs hold 97.9% of the animals. Kothi (Kinnaur) shows the same
pattern: 49 Rajput households own 2,416 sheep and goats, 13 Koli households own
72 and 6 Lohar households own 58. Most families herd their own flocks. Brahmaur hires pohals "by a very few
people in rare cases," and pays them free meals, clothing and wool, "two or
four sheep per hundred," and Rs 2 a month. In Kinnaur hired shepherds
(fawals) matter more. Kanum's landholders "found it difficult to engage
labourers to attend to their sheep and goats."

### Research question

> **How did the seasonal movement of flocks between high summer pastures and
> low winter grazing shape life in the Himachal hills between 1881 and 1961,
> and who controlled and profited from it? As the raja's grazing rights
> passed to the Forest Department and roads reached the valleys, did the
> herding cycle persist as an adaptation to mountain and season, or was it
> reshaped by who owned the flocks, the pastures and the labour of hired
> shepherds?**

Sub-questions:

1. **The cycle.** What is the herding calendar in each village (departure,
   summer grazing, return, winter grazing), and how far do the routes and
   months in 1961 match those in 1881 and 1910?
2. **Ownership and labour.** Who owns the flocks, by caste and household? Who
   does the herding, and how are hired shepherds paid? Who owned the pastures
   and collected the dues before and after 1948?
3. **Change by 1961.** What happened to the flocks under the permit system,
   and what do the surveys connect with change: permits, roads, road-building
   wages, the 1962 border closure, or orchards?

## Interpretive framework

### Step 1. Annales: geography, time and comparison

The Annales step places the herding economy in space and in Braudel's three
time scales, and compares cases that differ in one feature.

**Geography.**

| Feature | What it does to herding | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Altitude | Fields stop at about 7,000 feet, and pasture continues above | "Above this altitude there is little or no cultivation" (1881, p. 275) |
| Snow and passes | Close the high pastures in winter and open them in summer. The Kugti pass needs "three days actually on the tops" | Newell, p. 18. Brahmaur, p. 11 (the Budhal rises at the Kugti pass) |
| Rivers | Set the unit of settlement: "a 40 mile length of river between high mountains" | Newell, p. 10 |
| Ranges between valleys | The routes cross from one river system to another: Ravi to Chenab (Lahul) in summer, Ravi to Beas (Kangra) in winter | Brahmaur, p. 55. Newell, p. 36 |

**Time.**

| Scale | What stays or changes | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Longue durée | The mountain, the snow line and the route | The same months and routes in 1881 (1881, p. 275), 1910 (Rose, p. 297), 1931 (1931, p. 142) and 1961 (Brahmaur, p. 55) |
| Conjunctures | Four periods, each with its own control over pastures, labour and exchange (see Step 2) | |
| Events | The 1948 merger, the post-1948 permit system, the 1962 border closure, the first jeep road | Newell, p. 19. Kanum, p. 51. Chitrari, p. 24 |

**Comparisons.** The Annales step compares cases that share the mountain year
but differ in one feature:

- **Two valleys.** The Ravi faces south to Kangra, and the upper Sutlej leads
  to Tibet. The full comparison is in
  [two-valleys-sutlej-ravi.md](two-valleys-sutlej-ravi.md).
- **High and low villages.** Brahmaur keeps 45.3 sheep and goats per household
  (our arithmetic, Brahmaur, p. 54). Lower villages keep 1 to 6 but buy
  animals from passing herders (Bir, p. 43. Shakrori, p. 75).
- **Herders and settled farmers in one valley.** Brahmaur's Gaddis move their
  flocks themselves. Devi-Kothi sends its sheep "with professional graziers"
  (Devi-Kothi, p. 48).

### Step 2. Marxist: contradictions within each conjuncture

The Marxist step takes each conjuncture found in Step 1 and asks which two
forces inside it pulled against each other, and what new arrangement came out
of the conflict.

| Conjuncture | The contradiction | Evidence | What it produced |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Two sovereigns, c. 1846 to 1947.** Gaddis hold land in British Kangra and in Chamba State | Two rulers claim the same herders. The Gaddis used to pay a fine to Chamba whenever Kangra fined them | "whenever fined by the Kangra authorities, to pay a similar penalty into the Chamba treasury ... our institutions have taught them greater independence" (1881, p. 275) | Herders who settle "all the year round on this side of the range acknowledging no allegiance whatever to Chamba" (1881, p. 275) |
| **The raja's pastures, to about 1950** | The raja owns the grazing and the shepherds use it. The raja sells the right to collect fees, and the buyer is "usually a Gaddi", so one Gaddi collects from the others | Grazing rights "belonged to the rajah of Chamba", fee rights "auctioned ... to the highest bidder" (Newell, p. 18). An official "received wool in lieu of revenue" (Newell, p. 14) | A class of fee-farming Gaddis inside the herding community |
| **The Forest Department, 1948 to 1961** | Conservation against subsistence. The state closes pastures to stop erosion. The shepherds need the grazing | Passes issued because "intensive grazing for generations led to erosion" (Chitrari, p. 23). Closures cause "a sense of resentment against the Forest Department" (Kupha, p. 136). "Forest Department do not now grant any additional permit" (Brahmaur, p. 46) | Permits become property: they "are freely bought and sold amongst shepherds so that shepherds have now become closed corporations" (Newell, p. 37). Fee-paying flocks fall 25.7% between 1952-53 and 1961-62 (Newell, p. 38) |
| **Roads and wages, 1950s to 1961** | The flock-owners need hired herders. Labourers now have road wages | Kanum's landholders "found it difficult to engage labourers ... Men and women now prefer to work on the roads rather than work as 'fawals'" (Kanum, p. 51). Road and building work pays "about Rs. 2" a day (Brahmaur, p. 45) | Herding labour moves toward the family, and wage work takes the landless |

Across all four conjunctures runs one standing contradiction: flocks are owned
by the landholding castes, and the Sipi, Koli and Lohar households own few or
none. In Brahmaur, Brahmans and Rajputs hold 97.9% of the animals (Brahmaur,
p. 54). In Kothi, Rajputs hold 94.9% (Kothi, p. 102).

The Devi-Kothi survey names the Baira Nala but not where it joins a larger
river. Placing it in the Ravi system is our reading of the map and needs
checking against the 1961 Punjab Census Atlas.

The river table adds a point about the herding routes. The home villages sit
in single valleys, but the routes cross from one river system to another:

- **Gaddi flocks** leave the Ravi valley (Brahmaur, Chitrari). In summer they
  graze in Lahul, on the Chenab side of the range. In winter they graze in
  Kangra, which drains to the Beas. The river systems for Lahul and Kangra are
  our addition, not the surveys'.
- **Kinnaur flocks** (Kanum, Nachar, Kothi) move down the Sutlej to the low
  hills of Bilaspur, Arki, Karsog and Sirmur in winter. In summer they go up
  to alpine pastures, which Nachar places in Lahaul and Spiti.
- **Tandi** sits on the Chenab at the Chandra-Bhaga confluence. Its people go
  south over the Rohtang to Kulu for winter work, which takes them from the
  Chenab system to the Beas.

For idea 10 this means the valley is the unit of settlement and farming, and
the mountain range between valleys is the unit of herding. Newell's river
units describe where people live. The routes show where their flocks eat.
The route map (method 2) should show both.

## Stage 3. Historical interpretation (plan)

The essay argues in two moves, following the framework.

1. **Annales: what stays.** Altitude, snow and the route fix the mountain year.
   The same months and routes appear in 1881, 1910, 1931 and 1961. The
   comparisons (high and low villages, herders and settled farmers, the two
   valleys) show geography setting the form of herding.
2. **Marxist: what moves inside the frame.** Within the fixed cycle, each
   conjuncture carries a contradiction: two sovereigns, the raja's ownership
   against the shepherds' use, conservation against subsistence, and flock
   owners against labourers with road wages. Each conflict produced a new
   arrangement. The sharpest is the permit, which began as a limit on grazing
   and became a saleable right.

The conclusion states which explains more of the change between 1881 and
1961. The evidence so far suggests that geography fixes the form of the
herding cycle, and the contradictions of ownership and state control decide
who gained and who lost within it.

## Section 5. Cautions

- **Grazing rights before 1948.** The 1881, Rose and 1931 texts say nothing on
  grazing dues or pasture ownership in the hills. The evidence for the raja's
  rights comes from Newell (1961) and the Brahmaur survey. The Kangra and
  Chamba settlement reports would fill the gap but are not in the source set.
- **1931 undercount.** The 1931 census was taken at the end of February, when
  flocks were in the low hills. Its occupation table counts 451 male
  shepherds and herdsmen in Chamba as principal earners. The Report itself
  says the migration affects the birthplace returns.
- **The fall in flocks.** Permits and the 1958-59 disease are two possible
  causes.
- **Scheduled Tribe date.** The Maingal survey gives 1956. `candidate-topics.md` gives
  1950 from a secondary source.
- **Newell's river.** Newell calls the Chandra Bhaga "Sutlej." The Chandra
  Bhaga is the Chenab.
- **Uncertain figures.** Maingal's livestock prose and table disagree.
  Chitrari's share of migrating households reads as 1.5% or 15% in the OCR.
- **Text reuse.** Rose's Gujjar passage copies Barnes (1881), so it is not
  independent evidence.
