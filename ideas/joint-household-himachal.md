# One hearth, one holding: the joint household in the Himachal hills, 1881 to 1961

Earlier versions: [candidate-topics.md](candidate-topics.md) (16 topics), [shimla-hill-states-sutlej.md](shimla-hill-states-sutlej.md)
(Shimla and the Sutlej), [begar-land-caste-12-villages.md](begar-land-caste-12-villages.md) and [begar-land-caste-5-villages.md](begar-land-caste-5-villages.md)

The sections follow the three stages in `project.docx`. Every quote was

### Terms used

- **Joint household.** Married brothers, or parents and married sons, living
  and farming together on undivided land. The 1961 surveys classify families
  as simple (couple and unmarried children), intermediate, joint, or other.
- **Fraternal polyandry.** Several brothers sharing one wife. The eldest
  brother marries, and the younger brothers are counted as her husbands too.
- **Polygyny.** One man with more than one wife.
- **Begar.** Unpaid labour owed to the state or its officials, mostly
  carrying loads.
- **Reet, dheri, haar, izzat.** Local names for payments at marriage, usually
  from the groom's side to the bride's family (bride-price).

## Stage 1. Research question

### The problem in the sources

Hill households in the Punjab and Himachal sources are larger and more often
joint than households in the plains. In 1881 Ibbetson measured persons per
family in villages. The figures below were read from the page image of
Abstract No. 18 (Results, PDF p. 47) and need a second check.

| Unit (1881) | Persons per family |
| --- | --- |
| Bashahr | 7.52, the highest in the province |
| Bhajji | 6.76 |
| Jubbal | 6.02 |
| Hill States, total | 5.06 |
| Keonthal | 4.68 |
| Punjab province | 4.66 |
| Simla | 4.29 |

In the same region, several brothers often shared one wife. Every source
layer records this, and every layer gives an explanation.

- **1881, Lyall on Saraj**, quoted by Ibbetson: "It is a matter of means and
  of land; a large farm requires several women to look after it. Where there
  is only one wife to several brothers, it will generally be found that some
  of the brothers are absent for part of the year working as labourers."
- **1881, Ibbetson**: "throughout the Panjab hills begar or forced labour is
  exacted, not by heads but by hearths; so that so long as brothers remain
  joint, they have to do collectively only as much begar as each of them
  would have to do if they separated."
- **1910, Rose on Lahul**: the villagers "say that their holdings are too
  small to divide, and that experience shows them that it is impossible for
  two sisters-in-law, with separate husbands and families, to live together,
  whereas two or more brothers with a common wife can agree."
- **1931 Census Report**: Bashahr and Sirmur show "a smaller number of wives
  than husbands," which "undoubtedly creates a suspicion of polyandry,
  particularly in the hill tracts." Bashahr had about 1,023 females per 1,000
  males in 1931 (our arithmetic from Table I), so the shortage of wives was
  not a shortage of women.
- **1961, Kothi**: villagers say polyandry has been "useful in keeping the
  family closely knit and in preventing fragmentation of small land
  holdings." "Out of 128 households only 18 cases of polyandrous marriages
  have been recorded."
- **1961, Kanum**: "the labour involved in tilling their small and scattered
  holdings coupled with their pastoral and migratory occupations need a
  number of workers ... Polyandry serves as a safeguard against division of
  the family and fragmentation of the ancestral property."
- **1961, Chaupal**: "At one time polyandry was fairly prevalent, but not so
  now ... the fragmentation of the holdings did not take place." At the time
  of the survey "there was just one polyandrous family."

The sources also tie household form to women's labour:

- **1881**: "in the higher Himalayas almost the whole of the field-work, not
  always excepting ploughing, is done by the women, the men working meanwhile
  as carriers, coolies, and the like."
- **1881, Anderson on Kulu**: "a land-owner as soon as he finds that he cannot
  till all his land with those already in his house looks out for another
  wife."
- **1931**: female workers per 1,000 male workers were 748 among Kanets, 796
  among Rathis and 799 among Dagis and Kolis, against 121 among Jats and 137
  among Rajputs of the plains (figures from the page image).
- **1961, Chaupal**: "Men have confessed that women work more, and if they
  stopped working, the economy of the family would be shattered."

### Research question

> **Why did households in the Himachal hills stay joint, in some villages
> through fraternal polyandry, and why was the practice declining by 1961?
> Was the joint household an adaptation to scarce mountain land, or an
> arrangement shaped by who owned the land, whose labour worked it, and how
> the state took revenue and begar from it?**

Sub-questions:

1. **Pattern.** Across 10 surveyed villages, how do household size, the share
   of joint families, polyandry and polygyny vary with land per household,
   women's field work and men's work away from the village?
2. **Explanations in the sources.** What causes do the sources give for joint
   and polyandrous households (land, labour, begar or revenue, harmony between
   women, custom, outside influence), and do the causes change between 1881,
   1910, 1931 and 1961?
3. **Decline.** Kothi, Nachar, Chaupal and Chergaon report polyandry declining.
   What do the surveys connect the decline with: education, wage work on
   roads, government service, the end of begar, or land reform?

### How the two theories read the same evidence

| | Annales reading | Marxist reading |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cause of the joint household | Physical structure. Small terraced holdings, a short growing season and mixed farming and herding need many workers and cannot be divided without falling below subsistence. The household form is a long-term adaptation. | Relations of production. The household is the unit that the raja taxed (begar "by hearths") and the unit that owned land. Women's labour is the main farm labour, and bride-price and polygyny treat it as a value that can be transferred. |
| Time scale | Long term. The same explanation appears in 1881, 1910 and 1961. Change comes slowly with roads and schools. | Change follows the economy. When men can earn wages on roads or in government service, the household loses its reason to stay joint. |
| Key evidence | Fragmentation of holdings named as the cause in Kothi, Kanum, Rajana and Chaupal. Bashahr, the most mountainous state, has the largest families in 1881. | Anderson (1881): women "valued only as they are useful for labour in the fields." Polygyny for "additional help for agricultural operations" (Kothi, Nachar). Anderson on Lahul (1881): polyandry dying out because "the male population has now many new ways of earning a living." |
| Test that separates them | Does household form track land per household across villages? | Does the decline track wage work and service, and did the end of begar remove one incentive? |

Anderson's 1881 comment on Lahul already makes the Marxist argument: the
custom fades when men find "new ways of earning a living." The 1961 surveys
let the essay check whether that happened.

Patterns already visible:

- Every polyandrous village gives the same stated reason: preventing the
  division of small holdings and keeping enough workers together.
- Women do all field work except ploughing in almost every village.
- Bir has joint families without polyandry. Joint living does not depend on
  polyandry.
- Kanum's family-type table breaks joint families down by caste. It records
  29 joint families among 78 Rajput households and 12 among 19 Koli
  households. That allows a class comparison within one village.
- Chaupal's and Chergaon's low sex ratios come from government staff posted
  without their wives, not from the village population.

### Computational method

Each method has a Programming Historian lesson, as the brief suggests.

| Method | What it measures | Lesson on programminghistorian.org | Output |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Village dataset | The table above, checked against page images and extended with men working away and women's work | *Cleaning Data with OpenRefine* | CSV, 10 rows |
| 2. Coded concordance of explanations | Every passage in the four source layers that explains household form or polyandry, coded by the cause it gives: land, labour, begar or revenue, harmony between women, custom, outside influence | *Keywords in Context (Using n-grams) with Python*, *Counting Word Frequencies with Python* | CSV of coded passages and a table of causes by source layer |
| 3. Comparison across time | 1881 persons per family by state against 1961 household size in villages of the same state. 1931 sex ratio and wives per husband by state | *Visualizing Data with Bokeh and Pandas* or *Creating Interactive Visualizations with Plotly* | Two charts |
| 4. Text-reuse check | Whether 1961 authors repeat colonial explanations. Nachar quotes Lloyd, and Chergaon reprints earlier accounts of polyandry in an appendix | *Detecting Text Reuse with Passim* | A list of repeated passages |

Method 2 carries the argument. It shows whether the explanation shifts from
begar and revenue (1881) to land and labour (1961), which is the point where
the two theories disagree.

Method 4 is source criticism. If the 1961 surveys repeat Lyall and Rose, then
the agreement between layers comes from copying, not from independent
observation. The essay has to say which it is.

The draft script `scripts/kwic.py` covers the concordance step. Its term list
needs updating to household terms (polyandr, joint family, hearth, brothers,
fragment, reet, dheri, bride-price, polygam).

## Stage 3. Historical interpretation (plan)

The essay argues toward one of three findings:

- **Adaptation.** Household form tracks land per household, and the stated
  explanation stays the same from 1881 to 1961. This supports the Annales
  reading.
- **Labour and extraction.** Household form tracks the demand for women's
  labour and the unit of taxation. The decline follows wage work and the end
  of begar. This supports the Marxist reading.
- **Both, at different scales.** Land sets the limit over the long term, and
  changes in who earns what explain the timing of the decline. The data
  already point this way: every village cites land, and the decline appears
  where roads and service jobs arrive.

## Section 5. Cautions

- **Begar and the hearth.** Only the 1881 census states that begar was levied
  per hearth, and it gives this as Anderson's opinion. Rose ties begar to the
  head of household only in Spiti and Lahul. The 1961 surveys of Kanum and
  Chergaon say revenue and begar were "fixed according to the size of the
  holdings." The essay should present this as a disagreement between sources,
  not a settled fact.
- **Evidence by region.** Polyandry is concentrated in Kinnaur, Lahaul and
  trans-Giri Sirmur. The sample cannot speak for all of Himachal.
- **OCR tables.** The family-type counts for Rajana, Chaupal, Chergaon and
  Moginand are hard to read. Rajana's text says 21 polyandrous families and
  its table totals 19.
- **Tandi** contradicts itself on whether polyandry is more common in Pattan.
- **Kalath** has 8 households and cannot support a comparison alone.
- **1881 figures.** The persons-per-family table was read from the page image
  and needs a second reading.
