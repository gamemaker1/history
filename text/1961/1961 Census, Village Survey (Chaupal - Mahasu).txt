FOREWORD
Apart from laying the foundations of demography in this subcontinent, a hundred years of the Indian
Census has also produced 'elaborate and scholarly accounts of the variegated phenomena of Indian life
sometimes with no statistics attached, but usually with just enough statistics to give empirical under-pinning
to their conclusions'. 1n a country, largely illiterate, where statistical or numerical comprehension of e"en
such a simple thing as age was liable to be inaccurate, an understanding of the soicial structure was essential.
It was more necessary to attain a broad understanding of what was happening around oneself than to wrap
oneself up in 'statistical ingenuity' or 'mathematical manipUlation '. This explains why the fndian Census
came to be interested in 'many by-paths' and 'nearly every branch of scholarship' from anthropology and
sociology to geography and religion'.
In the last few decades the Census has increasingly turned its efforts to the presentation of village
statistics. This suits the temper of the times as well as our political and economic structure. For even
as we have a great deal of centralization on the one hand and decentralintion on ths other' my colleagues
thought it would be a welcome continuation of the Census tradition to try to invest the dry bones of village
statistics with flesh-and-blood accounts of social structure and social change. It was accordingly decided to
select a few villages in every State for special study, where personal observation would be brought to bear
on the interpretation of statistics to find out how much of a village was static and yet changing and how fast
the winds of change were blowing and from where.
Randomness of selection was, therefore, eschewed. There was no jntention to build up a picture for
the whole State in quantitative terms on the basis of villages selected statistically at random. The selection
was avowedly purposive: the object being as much to find out what was happening and how fast to those
villages which had fewer reasons to choose change and more to remain lodged in the past as to discover how
the more 'normal' types of villages were changing. They were to be primarily type studies which, by virtue
of their number and distribution, would also give the reader a 'feel' of what was going- on and some kind of
a map of the country.
A brief account of the tests of selection will help to explain. A minimum of thirty-five villages was
to be chosen with great care to represent adequately geographical, occupational and even ethnic diver~ity.
Of this minimum of thirty-five, the distribution was to be as follows:
(a) At least eight villages were to be so selected that each of them would contain one dominant
community with one predominating occupation, e.g. fishermen, forest workers, jhum culti:vators, potters,
weavers, salt-makers, quarry workers etc. A village should have a minimum popUlation of 400, the optimum
being between 500 and 700.
(b) At least seven villages were to be of numerically prominent Scheduied Tribes of the State. Each
village could represent a particular tribe. The minimum population should be 400, the optimum being
between 500 and 700.
(c) The third group of villages should each be of fair size, of an old and sattled character and contain
variegated occupations and be if possible, multi-ethnic in composition. By fair size was meant popUlation
500-700 persons or more. The village should mainly depend on agriculture and be sufficiently away from the
major sources of modern communication such as the district administrative headquarters and business centres,
It should be roughly a day's journey from the above places. The villages were to be selected with an eye to
variation in term.s of size, proximity to city and other means of modern communication, neamess to hills,
jungles and m.ajor rivers. Thu~ there was to be a regional distribution throughout the State of this category
of villages. If, however, a particular dillLrict contained significant ecological variations within its area, more
than one village in the district might be selected to ~tudy the special adjustments to them

It is a unique feature of these village surveys that they rapidly outgrew their original terms ofreferenee, as my colleagues wanued up to their work. This proved (or them an absorbing voyage of discovery and
their infectious enthusiasm compelled mc to enlarge the inquirys scope again and again. It was just as well
cautiously to feel one's way about at first and then venture further a field, and although it accounts to some
extent for a certain unevenness in the quality and coverage of the monographs, it served to, compensate the
purely honrary and extra-mural rigours of the task. For, the Survey, along with its many ancillaries like
the survey of fairs and festivals, of small and rural industry and others, was an 'extra', over and above the
crushing load of the 1961 CenSus.
It might be of interest to recount briefly the stages by which the Survey enlarged its scope. At the first
_ Census Conference in September, 1959 the Survey set itself the task of what might be called a record in situ
of material traits, like settlement patterns of the village; house types; diet; dress; ornaments and foot-wear;
furniture and storing vessels; common means of transport of goods and passengers; domestication of animals
and brids; markets attended; worship of deities; festivals and fairs. There were to be recordings, of course,
of cultural and social traits and occupational mobility. This was followed up in March 1960 by two specimen
schedules, one for each house hold, the other for the village as a whole, which, apart from spelling out the
mode of inquiry suggested in the September, 1959 conference introduced groups of questions aimed at sensing
changes in attitude and behaviour in such fields as marriage, inheritance, moyeable and immoveable property, industry, indebtedness, education, community life and collective activity, social disabilities forums of
appeal over disputes, village leadership, and organisation of cultural life. Jt was now plainly the intention to
provide adequate statistical support to empirical 'feel', to approach. qualitative change through statistical
quantities. It had been difficult to give thought to the importance of 'just enough statistics to give empirical
underpinning to conclusion', at a time when my colleagues were straining themselves to the utmost for the
success of the main Census operations, but once the census count itself was left behind in March, 1961, a
series of three regional seminar in Trivandrum (May, 1961), Darjeeling and Sri nagar (June, 1961) restored
their attention to this field and the impcrtance of tracing social change through a number of well-devised
statistical tables was once again recognised. This itself presupposed a fresh survey of villages already done;
but it was worth the trouble in view of the possibilities that a close analysis of statistics offered, and also because the 'consanguinity' schedule remained to be canvassed. By November, 1961, however, more was expected
of these surveys than ever before. There was dissatisfaction on the one hand with too many general statements
and a growing desire on the other to draw conclusions-from statistics, to regard social and economic data as
interrelated processes and finally to examine the social and economic processes set in motion through land
reforms and other laws, lagislative and administrative measures, technological and cultural cbange. Finally,
a study camp was organised in the last week of December, 1961 when the whole field was carefully gone
through over again and a programme worked out closely knitting the various aims of the Survey together.
The Social Studies Section of the Census Commission rendered assistance to State Superintendents by way
of scrutiny and technical confment on the frame of Survey and presentation of results.

This gradual unfolding of the aims of the Survey prevented my colleagues from adopting as many
villages as they had originally intended to. But I believe that what may have been lost in quantity has been
more than made up for in quality. This is, perhaps, for ~he first time that such a Survey has been conducted
in any country,and that purely as a labour oflove.It has succeeded in attaining what it set out to achieve; to
construct a map of village India's social structure. One hopes that the volumes of this Survey will help to
retain for the Indian Census its title to 'the most fruitful single source of infonnation about the country'.
Apart from other features, it will'perhaps be conceded that the Survey has set up a new Census standard in
pictorial and graphic documentation.

Selection of villages for Socio-Economic surveys in Himachal Pradesh offered me an opportunity
to visit places which I could have normally not seen throughout my life and this was a delightful experience
which has left an indelible impression in my mind.
Chaupal was selected for survey some time in 196]. This is a multi-caste village predominantly
inhabited by Rajputs. Unlike other village Surveys its study would reveal certain aspects of life which are
different than those prevalent in the so called developed places which are nearer to towns. Except random
references in travelogues by Seven Heiden and Frazer, this is a pioneer ~ffort to present in some details the
socio-economic conditions of the people who have been living in the remoter part of our country. The
scope of enquiry was quite.large and any amount of investigation would,sometime leave one thing or the
other and [ had to send ~he Investigator more than once to this village to check up the lingering doubts that
I often had. This village lies at the foot of Chur-Chandni, a hill peak about] 2,000 feet high. Surrrounded by
thick and lovely forests the village bears a beautiful look. One of the main grouse which the people have
often expressed every time is that the area still remains cut off and they have not been lucky enough to get a
road link though about two decades have elapsed since the development works started in Himachal Pradesh.
When we took up this project the bus service was available upto Sainj only and a journey of 52 kms. had
to be_cQvered on foot. It is only recently that a fair weather bus service has been extended upto Deha, an
important intermediately vi_lIage between Chaupal and Theog.
This is a transitional period for the villagers here. They have of late become conscious of the deve~
lopment which is taking place in other parts of the Pradesh and some of them certainly feel that it is time
that they should exploit the resources at their disposal to get good return from their land which is ideally
suited for potato cultivation and apple plantation. Just across the ridge the farmers in Jubbal have
made trcm~ndous efforts in' Horticulture, but due to transport bottleneck this a.!:ea ha-s~laggedDehind. No
dOUbt SJm~ oft-hel'rogressive farmers among them have talken the lead and they are earning quite a bit from
app~e orchards. This is enough to give insentive to others.
If all goes well this will also be one of the best regions which shall undergo many changes-chan~es
for the betterment and prosperity. If people take up Horticulture as their brethern have done in Jubbal,

Kotkhai and Kotgarh they will soon catch up the so called pioneers.

I would like to convey my warm gratitude to all thsoe who rendered useful cooperation in bringing
out this monograph.
I hope this little piece of work, like others, will come up to the expectations of the lovers of the Census

publications.

The Village

Introduction
Chaupal is a small village lying at the foot of
Churi Chandni-a hill peak about 12000d ft. high
clearly visible from Simla towards east and covered with snow throughout the winter. The chaupal valley before the formation of Himachal
Pradesh formed a part of the erstwhile lubbal State.
The tehsil headquarters of Chaupal Tehsil are
located here. In spite of its being a village of
importance and in spite of the fact that the area
claims rich forests, Chaupal still has the distinction
of being the only tehsil headquarter in Mahasu
district not yet approachable by vehicular traffic.
The road to Chaupal from Sainj is under construction since long and the vehiculai traffic has been
opened upto village Deha only as recently as
April 13, 1967. Nowa regular bus service from
Simla to Deha runs which is suspended in rainy
season due to land slides on the newly constructed
road .. '\
When we took up investigation of Chaupal
sometimes in 1962 even Sainj was hardly connected
by vehicular traffic. One had to cover all the
distance from Sainj to Chaupal 52 Kilometres on
foot or on mule back. Now the way to Chaupal
from Simla runs through Theog, Chhaila and then
back again to Sainj by a side road where one has
to cross a newly constructed bridge over GiriGanga. This bridge is called Lilooh bridge. Then
there is yet another bridge over a tributary of
Giri-Ganga at a distance of about 2 miles from
Sainj. This bridge goes by the name of Bajroli
bridge. From then onwards the road up to Chaupal has not to cross any khad. The last lap of
journey from Deha to Chaupal, about 27 kilometres, has to be traversed on foot and the road
passes through magnificent deodar forest.
Previously before the vehicular traffic the entire
road used to hum with activity on account of
many mule caravans that carried good~ between
Sainj and Chaupal. There were halting places for
mules and multeeers at Sainj, Pipal bazar, Deha
and Chambi. Now the mules are steadily being
pushed off the road, as goods are carried at
cheaper rates by vehicular traffic. Sainj and Deha
are likely to lose importance soon when a
through bus service between Simla and Chaupal
starts running. The whole valley between Sainj

and Chaupal has attracted the covetous eyes of
planters and many have acquired lands at suitable
places and planted apple orchards. Some orchards
planted early have started bearing fruits. The
valley is reported to be well suited for apple
growing. Many more planters are expected to
reach Chaupal valley as soon as the road upto
Chaupal or beyond is constructed. At present lack
of transport facilities in the Chaupal valley have
to a great extent restricted horticultural activities
there although the climate and the land is reported
to be Very favourable for this venture.
The village gets a good snowfall in winter and
due to excessive .snowfalls the path gets usually
blocked up. In the monsoons, long stretches of
path become muddy. There is a sprinkling of
Government employees, shopkeepers and their
·families. Offices of the Divisional Forest Offiicer,
Block Development Officer, Tehsildar, Post Office,
Police Station are located at Chaupal. Their
buildings including that of Forest Rest House
are of modern structures, because the village has
been the seat of tehsil headquarters for quite
som'3time past. At one time this also used to be
the capital of erstwhile princely state.
The main castes residing h~re are Rajputs
Sunars, Kolis and Lohars w1th agriculture as
their main occupation. To supplement their income
some families do silversmithy, blacksmithY and
basket-making as their subsidiary occupations.
When Chaupal is linked with a good all-weather
motor road, it is expected to emerge out as a big
market for potatoes and apples.

Origin of the Village

.

It is not exactly known as to when the foundations of the village were laid, or how it derived
its name. According to the elders, same four
centuries back, a few _families of Thakurs used
to live here. The ruler of Jubbal state defeated
the overlord and annexed this territory. At one
time this remained the capital of the state and
the ruler used to live here during summer. Here
was a big fort till four decades back. Duirng
wazir Sher Singh's time there was a fire and the
fort was burnt down. A temple of Shirgul deity
has been erected at the site of the fort. A beauti-

The village is at 7,685 feet. It lies on the 77.5°P
longitude and 8l.0°F latitude. The nearby surrouding slopes are moderately steep and range in
elevation from 6,000 feet to 9,000 feet.

So far the name of the village is considered it
is believed that the word Chaupal is derived from
the Hindi term 'Choupal', meaning a meeting place
or a somewhat flat portion of land. The upper
portion of the village is somewhat flat in appearance, therefore, this place seems to have acquired
this name.

Beyond Chur Chandni in the South lies tehsil
Rainka of Sirmur District while on the east lies
the minus area of Uttar Pradesh. Sub-tehsil
Kotkhai borders Chaupal in the northern direction. A wide panoramic view of the hill ranges is
seen from here. The majestic range of Chur Chandni at 12,000 feet, is about 26 Kilometres away.
This remains covered with snow for six months
during winter. In some of the hidden ravines of
the peak, snow is found throughout the year.
Many a villager and some outsiders visit the Chur
peak during the pleasant months of summer. 1 hey
pay homage to Shirgul deity whose temple is situated at the foot of the peak. Arrangements for the
pilgrims to stay are available. One passes through
some of the most beautiful forests beyond village
Sarain while going to Chu[ peak.

Cremation Ground
Swarans and Harijans have separate cremation
grounds. Brahmins and Rajputs being quite near to
Nikoda stream, cremate their deads on the bank of
the stream. Harijans take the deads to N och-ni~
dhar, half a mile upward and quite near to the for~
est rest house and cremate there. Children below
ten years are burried. Lepers are also burried.

The place is known as Jorna.

Water Sources
The only source of water is Dhadhoo nala, half a
mile down on Chaupal-Sarain path. Some water
gushes out of this spring. During dry months of
summer the quantity of water decreases. A long
queue of the villagers are seen in the days of scarcity. Sometimes people land into scuffles. Another natural source is the Takhnol spring
which lies in Bhodha circle. This does not benefit

the people of the village because of the long distance and very little quantity of water. In summer
it virtually dries up. The third spring is known as
Bothla lying in Manjotli circle. A few households
from Chaupal use this water.
Numerous surveys were conducted for the regu~
lar supply of water. But villagers with great regret
inform that none of these surveys materialised. The
two small perenial streams from where the water is
proposed to be brought are, Sadachh and Bachla
nearly 12 and 5 Kilometres away.

Place of Worship
There are five places of worship in the Village.
The first is of Launkra Bir deity. Its temple is
situated near the rest house when one enters Chaupal. It is said that this diety protects the lives and
property of the villagers. The second small temple
of Shiva is at the peak known as Gadal, 2 kilometres away. A small new temple is under construction. Two small temples of Narsingh Bir and Gugga
Pir deities are situated in ChaupaI. Pir is a deity of
the Scheduled Castes and is held in high esteem.
In its temple are placed iron chains and a rod.

In the middle of th(! village is a temple of Shirgul
Devta. Comparatively speaking 'it is a big three
storeyed wooden building. The wooden carvings
on the outer part stand eloquent testimony to the
skill of crude crafts-men. At the entrance are the
carVings of Sahayoks the guards carrying naked
swords. There are different motifs of animals and
ornamental designs. There is a big courtyard attached to the temple where the religious ceremo.
nies are held.
An important legend is associated with the
temple. It is said that 'Shirgul' was the son of a

now in Sirmur district. He was a great worshipper
of Lord Shiva. Having been pleased with devotion
Lord Shiva endowed him with great pOWer. During

ropes. Then he set out towards the Himalayas to

House Type

Houses here are influenced by the traditional
designs generally found in Chaupal Tehsil. Cold

climate, has its own effects. Leaving aside the
Government buildings, villager's houses are made
of stone and timber. No mortar is used. For the
roof, slates are used. The houses are double storeyed or may even be three storeyed. The doors
are low. The timber is more and frequently used
bec,ause it used to be easily and cheaply available.
The Government buildings are two storeyed. A
few of them have G. I. sheets and wooden planks
roofing. The roofs are gabled in shape and rooms
are well ventilated.
Recently Giaru Ram has built a four-storeyed
house on a modern design.
Material :-The materials used are stone, slates,
and timber. Stone is collected from the nearby
stone quarries after obtaining a permit from the
Forest Department. The mason dresses them
either at the quarry or at the site. For slates too,
permits are obtained from the Forest Department.
Then these are extracted from the slate quarries
located in the nearby grazing pastures. The best
slates in Chaupal Tehsil are found in Rupadi and
Mattal quarries about 13 kilometres away. It
is reported that slates from these two places are
even carried to Rainka Tehsil of Sirmur District
and to villages in Uttar Pradesh.
There is no scarcity of timber in the village. It
is available at Zamindari rates. These rates are
very cheap and a deodar tree may cost about a
rupee. For obtaining the timber, villagers apply to
the Forest Department through the village Panchayat. Usually the number of trees recommended
by the Panchayat is sanctioned by the Forest
Department. Iron bolts and nails are purchased
from local shopkeepers at exhorbitant rates.
Labour:-There are two ways of collecting the
material for the construction of a house. The traditional way is to collect the material with mutual
help. This is known as daroch. For it, the owner
who wishes to construct a new house calls his
relatives and close neighbours. He would explain
his plans of construction. The men gathered would
Volunteer to help in collecting the material like
stones, slates and timber. At the night, they are
served with food like sidoo and ghee. A rich man
may even slaughter a goat to be served to the
villagers and the workers. Wine is served.

The second way of collecting the material is
that the owner may engage labourers and make
cash payment. Sometimes the wages are paid in
kind. The owner engages the skilled labour at
the slate quarry or in the jungle from where the
materials are to be brought. The Stones and
slates are dressed and the trees felled. These
are Sawn into beams and planks.
In the past, contract for the construction of the
house or for each storey was given to a mason.
The contract value used to be in the past between
Rs. 200 and ~OO for a double storeyed house
having four rooms. Or it used to be about Rs.
180 for each storey with two rooms. The amount
could be paid either in cash or kind. But now
daily paid labour is engaged. by the owner. A
mason or carpenter noW charges Rs. 5 per day
and an unskilled labour is paid Rs. 3.50.
The village carpenters and blacksmiths are there
to assist in designing the wooden frames. The
unskilled labourers are available in Bodhna and
Manjotli villages.
Foundation:- The foundation of the house is laid
do_wn on some auspicious day fixed by the
pnest. Even, the mud of the, site at which the
bouse is to be built is shown to him, lest it be
haunted by some evil spirits. Thc site is levelled
and cleared of all the stone boulders. The foundation is dug up to about 3 feet..At the four corners
of the foundation, four stones are placed. The
priest lights earthen lamps over some grains at the
corners and conducts a brief worship. It is after
this, that the work on the foundation is started
with dry masonry work. When it reaches the
ground level, further masonry work is started
according to the thickness of the wall. Generally
no plinth is kept. Juggery is distributed at the time
of laying the fuondation, Dhani Ram informed
that the foundation till its completion is watched
lest anyone may throw some charmed article
into it. This can harm the owner of the house or
his relatives.
Ground Floor:-The doors of the lower storey
are placed when the wall is about a foot high from
the ground level. The doors are placed at an
auspicious time. Jaggery is again distributed
among the gathering. Generally the doors of
the houses are found facing towards the East or
South depending on the sunny aspect of tee area

No other specific reason could be found. Dry
masonry work is carried right up to about six
seven feet. Dressed stones are used on both sides
of the walls and the gape is filled in with un even
stones. Small nitches are kept in the walls.
Stout horizontal wooden beams are placed in the
wall after everyone foot during the construction
work. The wooden beams are used to ensure the'
durability of the house. These keep the house
warm. When the wall reaches a hieght of about
6-7 feet, long thick wooden beams of 6 X Y are
placed over them. These beams may be projected
by about 6 feet from the wall to one direction for
supporting the verandah. Wooden beams of about
6-6 feet long or inserted to the other sides of the
wall if the verandah is to be laid on more than
one side. These are kept at the start of the work
on second storey, in a horizontal level with the
projected long beams.

The ground floor is used as a cattle shed. If
it has two or three rooms the spare room
may be used to keep agricultural implements or
for storing household goods.
Upper Storey:-After filling the gaps in between
the wooden beams, further construction of the
wall is carried upto about six feet. Space for
small nitches and for wall, almirahs and windows
are kept during construction. The opposite smaller
walls are raised to form a gable for laying the
roof. The rooms in upper storey are used as
kitchen and sleeping room.
Roof laying:-On the top of the gabled smaller
walls, a stout long ridge beam ~'placed. After
this, wooden poles are placed from the ridge
beam on both the sides. Their outer ends rest on
the gabled walls and are projected by about 4 feet.
The projection serves as a roof over the verandah.

Then from the ridge beam, long poles are kept
on both sides upto edges of the longer walls. The
distance between such poles are about two feet.
After laying the wooden structure in a gable shape,
work on spreading the roof is started. It is carried
from the ends to the ridge beam. First of all two
layers of slates are nailed into the wooden structure. These may be of bigger sizes of about 3
x 4 feeL The slates in upper layers are of smaller
sizes. At the ridge, an over-lapp1Ilg -of slates from
the opposite side is arranged in order to\ protect
from any leakage. Round vents are kept in the
roof which operate as sky light. During rains,
these are closed with slate.

Ceiling:-Before laying the roof, horizontal
wooden beams are placed over the walls of the.
upper storey. The ends coincide with the outer
ends of the gabled beams. In these horizontal
beams wooden planks are nailed. The planks are
thick. In the corner, an inlet of about 2' X 21'
dimension is left for entering into the space between the roof and ceiling, used for keeping
household articles.
Floor:-The ground floor is first raised by about
6 inches by filling small bits of stones. Then a layer
of flat stones are placed to give the ground a
finishing touch. The floor of the second storey
is made of wooden planks after these are thoroughly planned. At one of the cor~ers, a
rectangular entrance to the lower storey is kept.
This can be closed.
Doors, Windows and Nitches :-1 he doors are
small but made of thick planks. The dimension
is about 3' x 4'. Preferably one piece plank is used.
The sil of door frame is also very stout. The hooks
of the door are inserted into the holes on both
sides of the farme. From inside, the door can be
closed by means of a latch system. A handle and
chain are fixed from outside. Some houses have
round brass handles, with lion's head carving.
These are fixed to add beauty to the house. In some
of the modern fashioned houses the doors are of
full size, 4' x. 6' with glass panes.
A few houses have small windows. The~e are
provided in the upper storey. Dhani Ram's house
has bigger windows where glass panes have been
used.
Nitches are wood pannelled. Nitches are made
to keep the household articles. Similarly, almirahs
are pannelled with wooden planks.
Verandah :-Four to five feet wide wooden
verandah extends to two or three directions. It
rests upon tne horizontal wooden beams over the
walls of the first storey. The verandah, of course,
is without any ceiling.
The way to the upper storey is by wooden staircase constructed along the wall. It reaches the
verandah which leads to rooms. The stair-case in
some cases has been made of a circular log of
wood, in which nitches for steps are cut.

Compound:-In front of the house a circular
compound is made. A low parapet wall is constructed all around the platform and the floor is
laid with plan stones. This serves as a barn for
different agricultural operations. Social functions
are held in the compound.
Completion:-After the above stages are complete,
the rooms in the upper storey are ornamented from
inside. Half the walls from the bottom, are panneUed with wooden planks. These have carvings of
flowers from outside. Even the almirah flaps have
carvings. Timber is used to keep the rooms warm
and to impart a touch of beauty. About 1 inch
.thick mud plaster is applied to the walls from both
sides. When it gets dry a 'coat of cow-dung is
given. In the last, white washing is done. It is
then regularly done twice a year before important festivals.
There are no bath rooms. During summer, men
bathe at the spring. Women bathe in open on one
side of their hOllses. In the verandah in front of
the kitchen, a projected platform of about 6
x 4 feet is raised by 2 feet. It is used for cleaning
utensils and children are given a bath there.
The kitchen is separate and kept very clean.
The hearth is made in one corner at a little raised
space. There is an opening in the ceiling for smoke
.outlet.
Except government buildings, the houses do not
have latrines. People go outside in the open.
The characteristic feature in the houses found
here is that the floors of the second storey ang
verandahs are kept very clean. Women are seen
rubbing the floor with kanslz grass soaked in
water. Stalks of kalvni millet are used.

Kothur:- This is a pucca store room for keeping
grains. A few houses have separate kothars, built
adjacent to the main house. Four or more compartments are made1in it for keeping different
grains. Some households have kothars made in a
room in the lower storey. Separate grain stores
are safe in case a fire breaks out in the house.
Housewarming:-When the house is ready the
last ceremony of house warming takes place. The
priest performs a brief worship of the local deity.
The owner throws a feast to the near and dear
relatives. Others are given jaggery.

Birth Customs
Some of the elaborate birth customs are given
here. Restrictions are imposed on the work to
be done by pregnant women. She is not permitted
to fetch wather or fuel from the jungle.

Bandejh -On the advice of the priest, the parents
rbay make a promise to the family deity for the long
life of the child. This is known as bandejh.
After a couple of years on an auspicious daY,hawan
is performed. The family deity is sometimes sent
for. A grand feast is given to relations and friends.
J

She is always given nurishing deit like milk,
ghee and meat and the food she likes.
Delivery -The older women of the house help
in the delivery. Elderly women of the neighbourhood may also assist. The trained midwife is called
from the hospital. The delivery takes place in the
lower storey which is kept clean. Sometimes help
from an experienced women belonging to Scheduled Caste is sought.

The umbilical cord is cut wit.h a sharp knife and
placenta is burried outside the house. The mother
and the room in which the delivery takes place
are considered polluted for a couple of days.
If a son is born, there is great joy. Guqs are
fired. Jaggery is distributed among those present
and friends. The happy news is sent to relatives.
The midwife if engaged gets Rs. 5 and a piece of
cloth. The woman from the neighbourhood who
helps in delivery is given -Re. 1 and grains.

Villagers come and offer blessings. They offer
a little bunch of doob grass and a rupee to the
father. He returns the ruppee to the individual
with an additional amount and little jaggery.
After delivery the child is given a warm bath and
the child's ears a;e gently pulled to ensure that the
child is not dumb. The birth time is noted and
communicated to the family priest. Usually no
horoscope is prepared by the priest but the time is
noted in his records which may be of some help at
a later stage.
During the first fortnight the mother is given
very light food like wheat nishasta prepared in
ghee and soonth. Milk is given in considerable
quantity. Better off families give almonds and
dry fruits.
Sutak -After 21 days, the unclean period is over.
The mother may be taken to the upper storey
earlier. On this day, Panch Garbi hawan is performed and the priest is given a little case and grains.
Am.ong the Scheduled Castes the hawan is not
performed.

Nam Karan-The child's name is ~ither given by
the family priest or by the eldest member of the
family. It is usually given any day after nine
months or one year. Old types of names are not
favoured now. Dhani Ram has hamed his children Jagmohan and Asha, Karam Das has given
names like Ramesh and Brij Mohan. The old
types names are no longer popular. The child is
taken outside the house after about two months.

Anna Prashni-When the child iEi about 8-9
months old, solid food is given to the child for the
first time. Before this, the child is fed on breast
and cow's milk. On this day, a hawan is performed
by the priest. The child lies in the mother's lap
and with the help of one rupee silver coin kheer is
touched to the tongue. This ceremony is observed
by Rajputs, Brahmins and Sunars. A thread IS tied
in the wrist of the male child. This is known as
Dhali.
Mundan-This ceremony is held in the 1st, 3rd,
5th or 7th year. This means shaving off the head
of the child by the barber who is paid Re. 1. A
light feast is given to relatives and villagers. sThi
ceremony is performed by Rajputs and Brahmins.
Some families of Sunars and Tarkhans perform
this ceremony at the Chyr peak. They prepare
halwa and offer cash before the deity.

Janeo-Sacred thread is worn by Rajputs and
Brahmins only. The priest gives an auspicious
date and a simple ceremony is held. Guru
mantra is whispered. into the boy's ears when
sacred thread is worn. He begs alms. The priest
is given about Rs. 5 and some grains, while a
feast is given in the afternoon.
Boring of ears and nose~Girls get their ears and
nose bored when they are about four years old.
This is done with a needle or a thorn. The ears
and nose of boys are bored if they' are under thn
influence of bad stars.
Superstitions-Barranness of a woman is due to
wrath of some evil spirits so say the orthodox and
conservatives of Chaupal. 'They consult the oracle
of their family deity and perform the ceremonies

Marriage Customs
Of all the celebrations, marriage is the most
important and colourful ceremony. The marriageable age for boys is between 14 to 16 and for girls,
it is from 10 to 14. years.
Parents start becoming anxious and start the
negotiations about getting their children married,
when they are passing through their adolascence.
Gradually a search is made within the community
in near by villages. Once a broad selection is made
the proposal is made to the girls parents from the
boys side, by the m'lternal uncle and other villagers.
The girls parents make direct enquiries about the
economic means of the boys family and other
matters in which they are interested. The family
priest is consulted. He sees they do not belong
t6 the same gotra and are not influenced by bad
stars. If every thing goes well a date is fixed
for the betrothal. In some cases, the girl's parents
have a look at the boy before they finally agree.
Sai -At the time of engagement, the boy's father
gives the girl the following articles;Chak I
Tilli ~ Ornaments in gold or silver,

Batna--Is rubbed on the bride and bridegroom

If a party of village

On reaching the bride's house the party is well

Thcy are served food twice.
Vedi-The marriage celebration takes place in a

This i5 known as Vedi. The long banana stalks

haven. The god Ganesh is worhsipped. The

The priest ties the bride's, chadar with a loose
cloth or Choga of the groom and takes the
couple seven times round the sacred fire. He
keeps on chanting the mantras. And thus the
marriage is solemnised.
Dham-The villagers are also invited by the
bride's father to the wedding feast given in honour
of the marriage party. The feast consists of rice,
pulses, potatoes and halwa. Shallar and ghee
are important items of the feast.
A rich family may also slaughter a goat to entertain the marriage party. Each household invited,
gives 50 paise or a rupee to the father of the bride
before taking the food. This is known as bartan.
It is noted down on a paper for the return hartan
at the time of the marriage in that particular
household.
Dowry -Simple articles constitute the dowry.
There may be a bed, some clothes for the bride,
a tin box and a few ornaments. Ornaments are
mostly of silver except a gold laung or finger ring.
On the return of the marriage party, the bride is
received by the women of the house. The bro~her

Jhejhda -This is common and simple type

of

On an auspicious day a party of about five men

Baratoo. The head of the party takes an ornament

The party stays for a night there. Next day they

A wedding feast is held. A goat may be slaughtered. Those invited give case presents to the

Reet-This is a form of marriage in which the

On the other hand the men may also be married
once or more times. Both the husband or the wife
can have a change of his/her choice. A married
woman may not like her husband. She may go
to her parents house and subsequently -decide to
get married to someone else. She conveys this 'to
her husband. They mutually agree to have a
divorce and the amount spent on the marriage is
given by the wife and ornaments given to her are

gives away the said amount to her husband.' This
is known as Chhoot. The wife gets in writing from
her first husband that the matter has been settled
and nothing is due form her. This is known as
Razinawan. It is after this that she is free to marry
again as and when an auspicious day is fixed by
the family priest. The marriage, takes place
according to biah or jhejhda form.

Haar-This is marriage by elopment The girl

some physical defect. The amount given for such
purpose is known as Dheri.
At one time polyandry was fairly prevalent, but
not so now. The reason given about polyandry
was that villagers favoured a joint family system.
There were less chances of quarrel in a family.
The men were ambitious for close·knit family. So
were women. The only factor which could keep
the brothers close was their common wife. Moreover there was less agricultural holdings which
could only feed smaller number of persons. Under
this system, the fragmentation of the holdings did
not take place.
But now, polyandry is on the decline. The
reasons are obvious. At the time of survey there
was just one polyandrous family of Shankru Chamar.
Ten cases of polygamy were found during survey.
Out of these, three men have three living wives to
each.
Death Rites
All the villagers in Chaupa] are Hindus. It IS
only the Government servents among whom there
is a Sikh and a Muslim. Therefore, we are just
giving the local customs among the Hindus. Children below ten are burried. Others are cremated.
When a person breaths his/her last, he/she is made
to lie on the ground. The ground is given a coat of
cow-dung and a plain cloth sheet is spread. A
person is not made to die over the coat as it is
considered inauspicious.
Pm:;otan-Small pieces of gold or silver or
moonga, are put in the mouth of the dying person.
The dead body is given a wash and wrapped in a
piece of red cloth. This coffin is known as tala
or paaai.

Word is sent to the relatives and villagers
who start pouring in. They offer condolences to
the family. The elders at once prepare the bier.
This is of two kinds. One is an ordinary type
made in the shape of a ladder. This is known as
Ram Shed. Another type known as Facla-ku is a
decorated one made of carved wooden planks and
has arches. It is in the shape of palanquine.
In the meanwhile, a party of youngmen proceed
to the cremation ground to prepare the pyre, which

is about 7 x 3 feet.
The thick wood placed
length-wIse are known as Fai and those placed
breadth-wise are known as Shod. The pyre is
made of four layers.
The bier is takefrto the cremation ground, fo11o'\'.ed by the mourning members of the family and other
villagers. The counch is blown. If an old person
dies, coins are thrown over the bier. This is known
as kurbani. The bier is carried by the chief mourners or near relatives first and followed by other
villagers. Four persons act as pall-bearers at one
time. Hari-ka-nam-sat-hai and Ram-nam-sat-hai
are chanted by the followers. Women and children
do not follow.
At the cremation ground pinds-rice balls are
offered to the soul of the deceased. As a Maha
Brahmin is not easily available, death rites are
performed without him. If some one has lost
either of the parem!Y, he gets himself shaved at the
cremation ground. Thjs is known as Shee Bhadra.
After this the dead body is placed over the pyre.
Pieces of sandal wood are thrown over. Fuel is
again placed over the dead body. The chief mourner wohld then lit the pyre and the body is consumed
by the flames. An act of sanctity is performed
when the body is half consumed by the flames.
The head is touched with a long wood as a symbolic gesture. This is known as Kapal-Kriya. After
the pyre is considerably burnt, all the persons
return to their houses. On thjs day no food is
cooked in the house and it is supplied by the relatives and neighbourers.
The period of impurity is observed for 16 days.
On this day, a little hawan is performed an9 family
members start eating fried food. This is known as
Salsatri. The family priest will visit the house of
the deceased on the 21st day and after performing
some little puja, wm further purify the house. He is
giyen a rupee and grains. Upto the 21st day in
some households, Garud Puran, is read daily by the
priest. An oil lamp is kept, burning during this
period. Whenever a Maha Brahmin known as
Acharaj visits the house some belongings of the
deceased are given to him.
The ashes of the deceased known as Astu are
collected on the third day. Some take them to
Hardwar for immersion in the Ganga while a few
send them through registered parcel to the

Agricultural practices:-In Chaupal one finds
terraced fields. The agricllitural practices are
difficult. There are long and narrow fields. These
are not only small and narrow in size, but are also
scattered. Most people do not know about the
benefits of land reforms. Some of course welcome
this reform. The agricultural practices done 1D
the village around the year are given here.

Before commencing any field operation, an auspicious day is selected by the Brahmin. One
household selects a day and all other follow it.
Farmers carry their ploughs and drive the pair of
bullocks to the fields with the advent of sowing
season. Women carry manure to the fields in
groups. Sometimes men are seen carrying gheelas.
Manure is evenly spread in the fields. The edges
of the fields where the blade of the plough does not
reach are dug with mattacks. Sowing is usually
done by broad cast method. Villagers felt that
sowing by rows takes more seed and labour.
Women are employed to remove the different weeds
from crops. As the crop ripens the vigjJence is

Paddy-In the absence of irrigated fields,this
crop is sown on bakhalland in the month of Chaitra.
Usually this is sown on land kept fallow for one
year. After ploughing the fieJds three times, clods
are broken and the fields are levelled. In between
the ploughing, manure is spread. When the
fields are ready, the seed are sown in broadcast
method. When the plants are '"about ten inches
high, one round each of hoeing and weeding is
done. The harmful weeds are again removed after
about a month. Grass grown on relating walls
and edges is cut. -This serves as a foauer to the
livestock. Harvesting of this crop IS Gone III Asvma
and beginning of Kartika. The reaped crop IS
carried to barn in klltas where grains are removed
by beating with sticks. Threshing is also done
by bullocks who tread on the paddy ears. The
grain is then collected. Winnowing is done by
throwing it from wicker baskets when the wind is
blowing. Age old method of paddy busking with
the help of ukhal moosal is common.

sixteen years. A goat or sheep may live upto ten
years.

Tatooing
Men and women get themselves tatooed during
fairs. But there is local way of tatooing. Women
collect some un-ripe barley and make a paste of it.
The black soot of fry-pan is mixed with the paste
and is gradually pricked in the arms either with a
needle or with a thorn of Baikhli shrub. Tatooing
here is known as Kazal in the local dialect.
The different parts of the body tatooed are
fore-arms, hands, forehead, chin and thigh. The
word 'Om' and names are usually tatooed. Wristwatch flowers and birds are seen tatooed on the
forearms or wrists.

Beliefs and Superstitions
Villagers here are full of beliefs and superstitions. They are very afraid of the evil spirits.
In agriculture the first ploughing is started from
the direction of the moon in the sky. If it is in the
east, the ploughing bagins from an eastern end of
the field. In Bhadra, Sundays and Thuesdays are
'the good days for ploughing. In Chaitra, only Tuesday is considered good. If there is a bumpercrop,
a black stone is raised in the field so that the crops
are not destroyed by an evil eye. This is known as
Jhathawad. If one finds a ditch in a fields, it is a bad
omen. Toward off its evil effects, a goat is slaughtered in the field in the name of the family deity.
ILa snake is killed while ploughing, it is again a
bad omen. Two cobs in.a corn plant is still considered bad. From every crop a few grains are fixed
at the temple gate as a token offering to dei.ty.

Temple Management

The manager of the local Shirgul temple committee
is called the Bhandari selected and appointed by the
villagers of Chaupal. The Bhandari keeps accounts
of the income and expenditure of the Devta. The
diety has gold and silver ornaments in its treasury.
These things are kept in the possession of the
Bhandari.
Devta's income:-On Sankrant the first of every
Vikrami month villagers go to the temple and offer
cereals grown in their land and make some cash
presents. While some villagers bring goats. These
are slaughtened in the temple compound- after
these are accepted by the diety. offering of Re.
1 may be made to the diety. Other pilgrims
visit the diety and bring presents. While the Devta
visits different villages, the Bhandari collects gtains

and cash from each house. This is deposited in the

Amounts are spent on repairs of the building,

All the villagers are Hindus. Among the govern~
ment servants there may be Sikhs or Muslims.
When villagers wake up in tlle morning, they per~

fruits. On Shivratri, they may take meat.

Festivals and Fairs

According to Shri Trivedi, Superintendent of
Census Operations, Gujarat "Fairs and Festivals
are as old as mankind. They arise from man's
innate desire to congregate and divert himself from
~he humdrum routine of life. This universal fact
which has a deep psychological basis, has been
aptly described by the great poet Kalidas, when he
I

says," "~mqf>nH: lSf~1U1T" "Men are, by their
very nature, fond of festivals." Periods of activity
and hard work require to be alternated-by periods
of leisure and diversion, '\Vhich make mind and
body fresh and invogorated for renewed ·activity.
It is the institution of fairs and festivals which
provides such occasions by supplying this essential
need in human existence and by moulding the social,
cultural and religious life of the people."
