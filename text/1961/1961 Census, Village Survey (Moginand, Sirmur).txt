Foreword
A PAR T from laying foundations of
demography in this subcontinent, a hundred
years of the Indian Census has also produced
'elaborate and scholarly accounts of the variegated phenomena of Indian life-sometimes
with no statistics attached, but usually with
just enough statistics to give empirical underpinning to their conclusions.' In a country,
largely illiterate, where statistical or numerical
comprehension of even such a simple thing as
age was \iab\e tD be lnaCCulate, an understanding of the social structure was essential.
It was more necessary to attain a broad understanding of what was happening around oneself than to wrap oneself up in 'statistical
ingenuity' or 'mathematical manipulation'.
This explains why the Indian Census came to
be interested in 'many by-paths' and 'nearly
every branch of scholarship, from anthropology and sociology to geography and religion'.
In the Hist few decades the Census has increasingly turned its efforts to the presentation
of village statistics. This suits the temper of
the times as well as our political and economic
structure. For even as we have a great deal of
centralization on the one hand and decentralization on the other, my colleagues thought it
would be a welcome continuation of the Census tradition to try to invest the dry bones of
village statistics with flesh-and-blood accounts
of social structure and social change. It was
accordingly decided to select a few villages in
every State for special study, where personal
observation would be brought to bear on the

interpretation of statistics to find out how
much of a village was static and yet changing
and how fast the winds of change were blowing and from where.
Randomness of selection was, therefore,
eschewed. There was no intention to build up
a picture for the whole State in quantitative
terms on the basis of villages selected statistically at random. The selection was avowedly
purposive, the object being as much to find
out what was happening and how fast to those
villages which had fewer reasons to choose
change and more to remain lodged itt the past
as to discover how the more 'normal' types of
villages were changing. They were to be primarily type studies which, by virtue of their
number and distribution, would also give the
reader a 'feel' of what was going on and some
kind of a map of the country.
A brief account of the tests of selection will
help to explain. A minimum of thirty-five
villages was to be chosen with great care to
represent adequately geographical, occupational and even ethnic-diversity. Of this minimum of thirty-five, the distribution was to be
as follows:
(a) At least eight villages were to be so
s~lected that each of them would contain one
dominant community with one predominating
occupation, e.g. fishermen, forest workers,
jhum cultivators, potters, weavers, salt-makers.
quarry workers etc. A village should have a
minimum population of 400, the optimum
being between 500 and 700.

\'1

(b) At least seven villages were to be (If
numerically prominent Scheduled Tribes of
the State. Each village could represent a particular tribe. The minimum population should
be 400, the optimum being between 500 and
700.
(c) The third group of villages should each
be of fair size, of an old and settled character
and contain variegated occupations and be, if
possible, multi-ethnic in composition. By fair
size was meant a population of 500-700
persons or more. The village should mainly
depend on agriculture and be sufficiently
away from the major sources of modern cortI"-,
munication such as the district administrative
head-quarters and business centres. It should
be roughly a day's journey from the above
places. The villages were to be selected with
an eye to variation in terms. of size, proximity
to city and other means of modern communication, nearness to hills, jungles and major
rivers. Thus there was to be a regional distribution throughout the State of this category of
villages. If, however, a particular district contained significant ecological variations within
its area, more than one village in the district
might be selected to study the special adjustments to them.
It is a u;ique feature of these village surveys
that they rapidly outgrew their original terms
of reference, as my colleagues warmed up to
their work. This proved for them an absorbing voyage of discovery and their infectious
enthusiasm compelled me to enlarge the inquiry's scope again and again. It was just as
well cautiously to feel one's way about at first
and then venture further afield, and although
it accounts to some extent for a certain unevenness in the quality and coverage of the
monographs, it served to compensate the
purely honorary and extra-mural rigours of
the task. For, the survey, along with its many
ancillaries like the survey of fairs and festivals,
of small and rural industry and others, was an
'extra', over and above the crushing load of
the 1~61 Census.

stages by which the Survey enlarged its scope.
At the first Census Conference in September
1959 the Survey set itself the task of what
might be called a record in situ of material
traits, like settlement patterns of the village,
house types; diet; dress; ornaments and· footwear; furniture and storing vessels; common

The Registrar General, Shri Asok Mitra, has

My thoughts also go back to another very distinguished Administrator of his days as Census

Original Preface to the Report on the Census

Sir Denzil Ibbetson lays down "It has been
my endeavour to furnish such a sketch of the
salient features of native society in the Punjab
as will often supply the immediate need". This
reiterates the value attached to any census
reports.
In this brief monograph of ours, we give a
socia-economic portrait of Moginand, a village
lying on the road between Ambala and Nahan,
very near the border of Punjab. In all humbleness I place this before the reader. I had passed
through this village many years ago but I had
not stopped here and had a, little idea of what
was happening around. In autumn 1959 while
going through this village, we saw a great deal
of gaiety when a carpenter's son was getting
married.
He is an old carpenter and received us most
cordially. We spent sometime attending the
ceremony which was of a very complicated
nature to me. I knew very little of sociology
and much less of local customs. So it was one
lot of simple inquisitive questions which often
surpr~ed iand probablt'Y annoyed, thougjh no

opportunity gave me a chance to see many a
villages, of the Punjab which adjoin Sirmur
district.

thought it worthwhile writing 'on Moginand, a

Kalis-I53 Kalis reside in 27 households.
The word Koli seems to be used to denote almost all the low castes in hills. Some people
of this community call themselves as KoliRajputs and are reluctant to be called as mere
Kalis. They do not associate themselves with
other Scheduled Castes such as Chamars and
Chanals. They look down upon these. When
a demarcation line was drawn between
~;warans on one side and Scheduled· Castes on
the other, Kalis showed resentment and approached the Government that they may be
excluded from Scheduled Castes and be included in swarans. An agitation was carried
out and Kalis of Sirmur District joined hands
to form a Koli-Rajput Committee. This KoliRajput Committee of Sirmur District is affiliated with the. All India Koli-Rajput Mahasabha
of Ajmer. An All India Union of the Community wa~ established on 23rd March, 1937 at
Ajmer. The broad aims of this organisation
are confined to social reforms and economic
uplift. But soon, due to political advice, their
agitation cooled down and now they prefer
to be treated as Harijans for Government aid
but otherwise wish to be treated as swarans.
Many households are tenants. Others are
labourers who prefer to work on roads with
contractors. They are not well off. Their activities are the same as those of Rajputs. They
care little to improve and still are conservative in their outlook .

Chhappar-A certain amount of skill is required in constructing a chhappar though it
appears to be very simple. Khad grass is
abundantly available in the vicinity of the
village. Bamboo and maljin which are used
for making frame and to tie the grass so as to
keep it together, are also available. Khad grass
is cut from the jungles in December and January when it is dry. The women folk and
sometimes men collect the grass and tie it into
bundles which they bring home on their heads
or backs. The maljan is a long rope like creeper
and after being cut, is kept under water for at
least a week to make it more durable and
more elastic. When the material is collected,
the construction work of the roof starts. There
are also a few houses which have roof slope
to one side at certain angle to the wall. Once
the bamboo frame is fixed on the roof. the
grass is spread. Two layers of grass are spread
over the bamboo frame. The inner layer is one
inch thick and the outer layer is six to nine
inches thick. After laying the grass, it is pressed with long thin bamboo strips and tied with
ropes. The grass is laid over the roof by four
to six persons and thus work starts from
the lower portion upwards. Sometimes this is

Date of purification-The period of purification varies but as a matter of custom this is
held on odd days. Among Rajputs, it is observed either on the 11th or 13th day but in case

Havan is performed to purify the room. The

Brahmans and some of the old orthodox people do not take food or drink water in the

Nalwa cutting and other rituals-The nalwa

before the child is brought out of the house.
The loss of nalwa is considered to have evil

child and the mother. Clothes are preserved in
which the child is wrapped for the first time.
In some families of Rajputs, the mother shows
them to her son on the occasion of his marriage and reminds the groom of his obligation to
her.

omen.

ilpd tied round the 1)eck of the child.

the funeral procession go to the stream-side
to bathe and wash their clothes and then return
to their respective houses.

morning.

'Tenants
Tl1e tenants of Rani Kalandi Devi cultivate
land on batai system. In the past. under this
system separate small plots were put to auction
by the agent of the Rani and the highest bidder used to acquire these plots to cultivate
for a year. Tenants used to pay one third of
the total produce to the owner and collection
was made by the village Lumberdar. The
actual tillers of the land were affected by this
system which had resulted in low produce.
The result was that tenants never cared to improve the land by terracing or providing irrigation facilities because there was the constant apprehension of ejection or the land
passing on to the other tenant who might
offer a higher bid.
But now with the introduction of H.P. Abolition of Zamindari and Big Landed Estates
Act, the position has changed a bit in favour
of the tenants. The rate of batai system to be
paid to the owner in the first instance has been
reduced from one-third to one-fourth of the
total land produce. The tenants do not pay
any land revenue which is paid by the owner.
The crops are collected at the tenant's farm
from where it is removed by the Lumberdar
and taken to the landlord's house. The Lumberdar. receives one seer of produce against
one maund from the owner. The tenants have
now stopped paying batai to the owner from
kharif 1962, because they are being declared
owners of the respective holdings under their
CO?trol. The change of ownership would now
brmg about a change in the economic life of
the people. !hey would now put their heart
and soul to Improve their land and increase
the production.

Agricultural operations
Villagers first plough the fields. The seeds
are sown by broadcast. Then to cover seeds,
suhaga is applied. Hoeing and weeding is done
in groups by peasants. When the crops are
ready, harvesting. drying, threshing and winnowing are done. Threshing and winnowing
are done in the fields.
The Gram Sewak from the block side visits
the village and gives talks to the gatherings on
improved methods of cultivation. The villagers give him a patient hearing. The Gram
Sewak has also been laying demonstration
plots in agriculture. He takes_,._a small piece of
agriculture land of a peasant and on that area
gives demonstrations to the villagers in different techniques of agricultural practices. He
gives practical training in sowing, harvesting
and so on. About twenty villagers attend such
demonstration on agriculture and when the
crops are harvested he (Gram Sewak) compares
his produce with the produce of other villagers which is comparatively more than the
produce of the peasants. The villagers are
showing interest in the latest methods of sowing and cultivation.

Seeds

Villagers keep seeds' fdr the next harvest.
Poor families borrow seeds from better off

a bania fOT borrowing seeds because bania

developing interest for such seeds. Improved

seed. This is given to another villager on the

Manure
Cowdung is the common manure that the
farmers use. This is used after mixing with dry
and rotten leaves. In the earlier days, compost pits for depositing cow-dung were not
common and the manure was directly transferred to the fields or dumped in front of the
houses. But now things are changing a little.
Pits are dug for storing cowdung in the fields.
In these, compost is prepared. Manure is carried to the field in baskets or killas.
Chemical fertilizers are also being used. A
depot has been opened in the village and
Nirmal Das, local shopkeeper, sells these.

charming (jhadna) method. Occasionally the
affected place may be branded with red hot
iron bars. But this is done by a very experienced man.
Sukda-In this disease, goats and sheep
begin to get thinner and their hair fall and
aft;!r sometimes they die. There is no local
treatment for this disease.
Worms-Some young buffaloes develop
worms in the intestines. To cure, they are
given mustard oil or nila thotha or seera or
kungu mixed with lassi.
To encourage the farmers to rear and popularise good quality of cattle, one Hariana bull
and one Murrah buffalo bull have been kept
at Veterinary dispensary Kala Amb for breeding purposes.
The life span of a buffalo is about fifteen
years and that of a cow is 12 years. Cows and
she-buffaloes start giving milk at the age of
four or five. Bulls are put to yokes at the age
of four. Just to increase the appetite of cattle,
villagers give salt to cows qnd bullocks. About
1 /4th of a seer of salt is given to each animal
during a month. Cattles are kept in cowsheds during winter to protect them from cold
and under cover to avoid heat strokes during
summer. The average total yield of milk per
cow per day is from 2t seers to 4 seers and
that of a buffalo is from 5 seers to 7 seers.
Fishing
The Sailani and Markanda are in spate during rainy season and are full of fish. The
Government has imposed a ban on fishing and
no fishing can be done without obtaining a
licence from the Forest department. The
meagre catches by the villagers are consumed
by them and it is seldom that anyone sells fish.
During summer season the streams go dry
and there is no scope for angling and hence no
fish. Angling is done in three ways:1. During winter when water in stream
is clear in the morning, people fish
with trishul with an iron head. The
fish are speared with it and transferred to the basket.
2. Usual way of fishing . with hook and
rod is common.
3. During night, people burn bundles of
chi[ tree twigs and enter the waters

with a little sharp edged weapon
known as fadi. Seeing the light, fish
come to surface and are killed with
direct blows, and gathered.
Pastures
There are two kinds of pastures around the
village. One is a village pasture where the villagers have grazing rights. The second type is
a Government reserved area where no grazing
is allowed without obtaining a permit. The
permit fee is ann as -/ 6 / - per goat and Rs. 1/per buffalo. There is no fee for cows. One
can cut grass from Govt. pasture by obtaining
a licence for Rs. 1 /2/ - per year. One concession provided to villagers is that they can bring
one big bundle of grass from other charand,
by obtaining a permit on payment of anna one
to the forest department. In this way, many
people bring fodder to their cattle from far
away pastures.
Barber
Dhani Ram is the village barber. He is a
Koli. He cuts hair and shaves the villagers and
also attends to the people of nearby villages.
He receives a little money on marriages. While
shaving, he never applies shaving-soap to the
face and shaves simply by moistening the beard
and giving it a vigorous hand rub. The villagers
are unmindful of the cuts they receive during
a shave and the bleeding from such cuts is stopped by a liberal application of fine hearth ash.
Gharat
There is a gharati who resides on the bank
of Markanda and grinds the grains. His gharat
is run by the waters of Markanda. He charges
one seer of grains for everyone maund of
wheat or maize ground.
There are two Agarwal families settled in
the. village. They run retail shops and all articles of daily use are available with them. Important commodities available are salt, kerosine oil, cigarettes, tobacco, gur, dal, spices
and cheap mill-made cotton cloth. They sell
their articles with a high margin of profit.
Generally~ all transactions are made in kind
with the farmers and in cash with others. The
shopkeepers go on giving articles to the villagers on credit and at harvest time they realise
the amount in kind or cash alongwith interest
at the rate of 4 per cent. From other classes
they collect the arrears in instalments.

Hindu succession act
According to the traditional Hindu family
law, daughters could not inherit the property
of the father and only the sons were entitled
to the same. In the case of a man having
no c~ild, the property used to pass over to
the WIdow who could no~ sell it although she
used to maintain herself on the property.
After her death, the property was divided
among brothers of her husband or near relatives. In case there were two or more wives, the
property was divided> equally. Each part further
divided among sons of the same mother. In
case of minor children the mother acts as a
guardian so long as they remain minor. As a
matter of custom, illegitimate children are not
given any share in the property. There was no
dispute over the division of property. If some
one is married in a lower caste, he sinks to that
caste and his descendents are debarred from
inheritance, but he may keep the ancestral property. The present custom does not deprive
him of his property. A widow who is childless
can adopt a son and inherit the property. If
a widow remarries, she loses all the claim to
her former husband's property, though her
children have the option either to stay back or
go with the mother to her new home.

Untouchability

Untouchability and caste system has hold
in the village. About two decades ago, the
untouchables were kept aloof of all social or
religious occasions which were solemnised by
higher castes. Inter-dining and smoking between
the high find low castes were strictly tabooed.
Harijans would not sit together on the same
cot with any person of the high caste. But
gradually this is giving way. Now the position
is that Harijans enter the· temple at Trilokpur.
Some Kolis who are better off, than other families, sit and smoke together on the same cot,
though dining is still tabooed. The womenfolk of all castes go together to fetch water
from stream and men of lower castes participate in all social functions of Rajputs though
they still do not enter the kitchen. A few old
persons of high castes are adament not to
discard this practice and refuse to treat the
Kolis better.
This evil of untouchability is under-going a
gradual change. Practically all the households
know that untouchability has been abolishej under the law.

There have been in the past village organisations consisting of some sort of committee of
elderly members from all communities who
used to settle the disputes arising out of two or

ceremonies. Some elders were asked to interfere and to solve such petty disputes. Their
decisions were respected.
In Moginand similar practice existed before

the ·enforcement of the Panchayat Raj. The
members of the committee were respected by
both the parties at dispute and were also held
in esteem by the rest of the people. There was
.no distinction of any caste or creed for choosing any elder man of the village to the committee for settling the disputes. The only qualification for their selection was that they should
be men of intelligence, honesty, sincerity and
integrity. So far as the disputes between different bas were concerned, same were also
based on the foregoing lines. The matter wa.'i

hamlets. Generally the relations among the
different bas are cordial and rarely any dispute
used to take place.

5 Conclusion
Villagers live in jpint family system which
has been in a continuous process of formation.
Caste structure has close ties with village economics. The Inon-cultivating castes or households provide specialised services for the cultivators and receive food stuffs in return. The
quantity of food stuffs received from different
households for such different kind of services
depend upon the economic condition of a
particular fiousehold and the volume of service rendered in a year. This system of reciprocal obligation in village is known as
Zamindara kamana or Faslana. This is a traditional system which is still prevalent in village.
By this principle of give and take, the village
communities feel a sense of unity and oneness.

gives an idea to the people of the lines on
which democracy functions in the country.
The Panches have free discussions on any
measures to be adopted. All decisions in
Panchayat are taken on co-operative basis of
mutual understanding. All enjoy equal status
and can take suits to law courts in case Panchayat fails to bring the parties to any settlement.
Villagers receive incentive and are impressed
when a Government servant visits them. Men,
women and children gather round, talk and
follow him in and out of houses. But it was
a different picture about two decades back
when people were afraid of talking to a stranger in the village. Villagers do not lack the
spirit of hospitality and a, stranger has now
no problem if he has to stay for a c(,uple of
days or more. If one household has got no
beds for the visitors, the same are procured
from next door. They discuss any measure to
be taken up by the Government and weigh its
pros and cons. Usually it has been found that
people throw weight more towards such reform and accept it gladly.

On the communication side, the village is
inter-connected with key towns in Punjab and
Sirmur District have been connected by bridal
paths which are repaired by the P.W.D. as
well as by the village Panchayat. There is a
weekly postal s!ervice attached to the village.
Thus people have no difficulty in approaching
any part of the country.
In Panchayat, irrespective of any caste, sex
or creed, people have a right to vote and have
a privilege of being elected to the benches of
Panchayat. This brings people of different
caste of uneven economic classes together and
they exchange their ideas or mutual ben~fits
and interests. Village Panchayat commands
discipline through its Panches from the people
of village, which brings solidarity, unity and
oneness in the village community. Advantages
of new Panchayat over the traditional councils
are many-folds. It not only settles village .frictions but makes contacts with Government
bodies working on different development programmes and remind them of their functions
and duties. The working of Panchayat also

People have become political minded also

They can not be easily swayed away by any
political organisation except the one which
they think can give more benefits and com(l

give and take basis. Certain group of families

implements are taken on loan. There are other
occasions when villagers act together for common goal such as the building of bridal paths,

defence and common cause like locust control
or eradication of any epidemic, p~ople get
united.

a community acts as a symbol of unity and

The aspect of the house-The house must
never face East. But North and South are also
unlucky, as the north aspect brings poverty
and the south admits demons. Therefore when
a house, according to the 'nam-ras' rule ought
to face North, South or East, it is made to
face North-East or North-West, South-East or
South-West. .
TIMES FOR BUILDING

The auspicious moment-In Sirmur a handful of earth from the site selected is taken to
a Brahman, who predicts the auspicious
moment for laying the foundations, by declaring that a leopard, cow, fox or other animal
or drum will be heard at the appointed time.
The prophesy usually_comes off, because it is
made with due regard to local circumstances
at time, but if it fails. the time is postponed
and another day fixed.
Elsewhere in Sirmur, four jars containing
articles, brought from Hardwar or other sacred
place, are set at the four corners of the house,
and on these are laid the foundation stones.

Completion ceremonies
As the house approaches completion a
pirinda, a betel nut, and an iron ring, called
the three shakhs, are tied to a beam and to the
lintel of the door. The iron ring is a protection
against evil spirits.
Ceremonial decorations
Figures of deotas-In Sirmur, a house is at
once abandoned if the sign or image of a deota
is painted on it, in the belief that it has thereby
become sacred.
Ceremonial marks and signs
Bandarwal-In Sirmur, a bandarwal of red
flowers is tied all around the houses on the
first of Baisakh to invoke the blessing of Sri

Gul.
In Sirmur in Bhadon, a branch of tejbal is
kept at the door to avert evil spirits and dags.
A common variant is row of (probably
seven) cyphers under a line.
