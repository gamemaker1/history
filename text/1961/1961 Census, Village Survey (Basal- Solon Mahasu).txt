Foreword
Apart from laying the f01)ndations of demography in this subcontinent, a hundred years of the Indian Census has also produced 'elaborate
and scholarly accounts of the variegated phenomena of Indian life sometimes with no statistics attached, but usually with just enough statistics'
to give empirical under-pinning to their conclusions. In a country, largely
illiterate, where statistical or numerical comprehension of even such
a
simple thing as age was liable to be inaccurate, an understanding of the
social structure was essentia1. It was more necessary to attain a broad
understanding of VThat was happening around oneself than to wrap oneself up in 'statistical ingenuity' of 'mathematical manipulation'. This
expl~ins why the Indian Census came to be interested in many by-paths'
and 'nearly every branch of scholarship, from anthropology and sociology
to geography and religion'.
In-the last 'few decades the Census has increasingly turned its efforts
to the presentation of village statistics. This suits the temper of the times
as well as our political and economic structure. For even as we have a
great deal of centralization on the one hand and decentralization on the
other, my colleagues thought it would be a welcome continuation of the
Census tradition to try to invest the dry bones of village statistics with
flesh-and-blood accounts of social structure and social change. It was
accordingly decided to select a few viUages in every State for special
study, where personal observation would be brought to bear on the interpretation of statistics to find out how much of a village was static and yet
changing and how fast the winds of change were b'lowing and from where.
Randomness of selection was, therefore, eschewed. There was no intention to build up a picture .for the whole State in quantitative terms on
the basis of villages selected statistically at random. The selection was
avowedly purposive: the object being as much to find out what was happening and how fast to those villages which had fewer reasons to choose
change and more to rem",in lodged in the past as to discover how the more
'normal' types of villages were changing. They were to be primarily type
studies which, by virtue of their number and distribution, would also give
the reader a 'feel' of what was going on and some kind of a map of the
country.
A brief account of the tests 0'£ selection wiTl help to explain. A minimum of thirty-five villages was to be chosen with great care to represent
adequately geographical, occu:pat~ona~ and even ethnic diversity. Of this
minimum of thirty-five, the dIstrIbutIOn was to be as follows:(a) At least eight vi'Uages were to be so selected that each of them
would contain one dominant community with one predominating occupation, e.g., fishermen, forest workers, jhum cultivators, potters, weavers, salt-makers, quarry workers etc. A village
should have a minimum population of -leOO, the optimum being,
between 500 and 700.

Basal is a medium sized, multi-caste villagt 5 kilometres "from Solon near the Dyer
Meakin Brewery. It is two and half kilometres away .from the Kalka-Simla road
which touches the tip of the village at Chamba-ghat. Basal is sheltered behind the projecting rocks and lies on the gentle slope of
Karol mountain starting from the middle and
reaching right upto the foot of the Karol. It
sprawls over a large area. Basal the revenue
estate has eight hamlets. Jarash and Ber-kiser are on the far eastern side, touching
Chamba ghat; Kathiar and Bowra are in the
south-eastern direction; Kuthar and Deoli-kiser in the south. In the west is Gadhela and
in the north eastern side is Masri.
A number of villagers are working in Government offices at Solon and in the Solon
Brewery. Basal supplies fire-wood,
grass,
milk and ghee to Solon besides agricultural
products. Agriculture is the main occupation
subsisted by selling of dairy products. Other
occupations are blacksmithy, weaving, basket-making and pottery.
Basal is inhabited by ten castes, dominated by the Rajputs, who are believed to be
the original settlers. Together "With Brahmans, they form the upper class. T"Wo houses
are of Kumhars and three of Bhatras who are

N.E.S. Block with its headquarters at Jagjit
Nagar.
Basal has been selected for the Socio-economic survey as it is a medium sized village

result of the changes that have affected the

Inter-caste relationships
Rajputs, Brahmins and Kumhars mix
freely. Formerly the Brahmins observed
Chaul>:a strictly by changing clothes and
sitting for a meal in the kitchen and would
eat only a particular type of meal 'Whenever
they were asked for. But now this practice
has disappeared. However, they do not intermarry with others. They do not mix up
freely with Bhatras.
Cha:mars, Lahars, Kalis, Julahas, Doomnas
and Turis constitute the Scheduled castes of
the village. The Swarans consider Lahars
much nearer to them, than the others. The
Lahars are allowed to enter the houses of
Swarans except the kitchens. The Rajpu'fs
and Brahmans accept food and water touched by a Lahar. ·If in case a Lahar happens to
visit the house of a upper caste he is made
ta sit inside and is served food there alongwith others but he would clean his own
ufensi'ls.

The upper caste people allow other scheduled castes to sit in the verandah. They
are not allowed to enter the rooms and to
touch water and cooked food. In a social
function they are served meals in the
verandah. They sit in their own group,
slightly away from other castes and clean
their thalis themselves after the feast
is over. If persons of the upper caste visit
the homes of Scheduled Castes, they do
not eat the food cooked in their kitchens.
They will receive dry rations and cook their
meal themselves in the "open place or in
some fields and even earry their water.
However, they can sleep i.n a Lahar's house.
Among the scheduled castes' the Lohars
enter the houses of other scheduled castes.
They do not eat food cooked in their homes.
If they stay there they get dry rations and
then prepare their meals outside the house.
The Kalis; Julahas, Chamars and Doomnas
'Would not allow other scheduled castes to
enter their homes. In the sape way a Koli
would not allow a Julaha, a Doom or a
Chamar to enter his house. They will be
made to sit in the verandah and none of
them will accept food cooked by other
scheduled castes. They do not inter-marry
and strictly observe frame of their own caste
system.
If in a Scheduled Caste marriage people
of the upper caste are invited, the food, for
the marriage party will b..e cooked by the
Rajputs or Brahmins. Different castes 'Will
sit at one time, though the scheduled castes
wi'll form a row of their o'Wh. The cooks will
then serve food to the -whole gathering.

In a public eating house or a tea shop no
such distinctions are observed. A Rajput or
Brahmin will gladly enjoy his cup of tea
with a Koli or Doomna on the same table
and served by the same attendant. Thus a
beginning towards crossing of social barriers
has been made and it is expected that gradually this spirit is likely to permeate
through the social structure of the village
life. Of course to think abou't inter-caste
marriages is yet a moot point.

Joint family'
A joint family is one, where all married
sons and their wives live together wIth the
parents, and where mutual respect and good
behaviour unimpaired by the quarrels of the
women-folk prevails. In Basal, families of
this type are rardy found. It has been seen
that as long as only eldest son is married,
they live together. But when all the sons
are married' or. the parents die, the family
disintegrates and in the event of sub-division, the property is divided equally.'

Head of the Family-The eldest male is
the head of the family. He supervises and regulates the family affairs, He commands
great respect among the members of the
family and everybody obeys him.
Husband-wife reZationship--As the custom prevails, husband is regarded as superior to wife. The wife obeys her husband and
serves him sincerely. But this does not impair her status. She is looked upon as GrahLaxmi, the goddess of the home and is treated with love and respect. The harmony of
the home ,is maintained on the strong foundation of mutual respect and love between
the husband and the wife.

Both the husband and the w"ife never address each other by names, but by certain
code-words like Dh; Eh; Arey; or father or

Food habits
The staple diet of the villailers consists of'
what they produce Le. maize, wheat and
paddy. Maize is taken ,in winter and wheat
in summer. There are some households who
would like to have at least one principle
rice meal daily. The swarans produce su:('ficient wheat and maize grains which they

consume throughout the year. The Scheduled Castes who do not produce sufficient
grains are helped by the Swarns.
This is
done for the services rendered by them.
Since the conventional wages are paid in

kind, the Scheduled Castes collect their share
from every house after each harvest of wheat
:and maize or chapa ties at the tiines of festivals.
To study the food habits we may classify
the villagers as tenants and other persons
who are not do hard manual labour and are
engaged in
non-a~ricultural
professions.
Most of the land owners field labourers and
tenants eat meat occasionally. Whenever a
goat or sheep is killed as an offering to some
deity they take meat.
Every household rears live-stock, but milk
is sold in the market to supplement the income, and ,it has not formed a part of regular
diet. Children and old or sick are given milk.
Others may also take it. Otherwise the surplus milk is used for making butter and ghee
and it is the whey or butter milk ""hich is
extensively used. The villagers grow vegetables.
Villagers take tea in the morning. The
mode of preparation is very Simple. Water,
sugar or gur as the

case

may be, and tea

leaves are boiled together over the hearth,
and milk is then added. In the morning it is
a must for every body but there are some
people who are in the habit of taking it in
the after-noon also. Villagers are in the habit
of taking meals three times a day and these
three meals are locally called nahan, duppahari and blali corresponding to breakfast,
lunch and dinner, respectively. The nohari
or breakfast generally consists of maize or
wheat chapaties, left over of the evening
repast t with pulses or achaar or tea or lassi
whatever is available. Some takes sattus of
maize with lassi or chatni. This constitutes
their breakfast. The lunch may consist of
wheat or m,aize chaoaties, dal or some vegetables and lassi. Alternatively they may take
babrus with vegetable. The biaH or supper

is not very different from the lunch, and may
consist of wheat or maize bread, rice, pulses

Birth
The birth of a boy is more prized than the

For a month preceding the confinement,

When about a week is left, the local midwife keeps on visiting and massaging the

Gharats
There are six water-mills for grinding corn
all of them are fixed in the khud. One of
them js owned by Kishan Lal while the rest
are owned by the farmers of other villages.
These ghaI'ats are there for the last thirty
years. A channel leads the gushing water
into the base of the gharat, where the wooden
charkhies are fixed into an iron socket. The

charkhies rotate by the force of water a.nd
in return rotate the grinding stone. The graIns
are poured from the top into a hopper.
The gharati charges two seers of grains for
everyone maund of grains that are converted into flour.

Honey
Only a few houses keep bees. In the month
of Pausa or Magha, the farmers make a hole
from inside in a wall, bees make their hives.
Further, to provide entrance to the hive,
they connect it with a small hole from outside. It is plastered with cow-dung from
inside. The mouth of this hole is· covered
with a piece of wood or a small basket. It is
again plastered with cow-dung, so that the
smoke may not reach the 'bee-hive, and scare
away the bees.
To attract the bees, a piece of gu.r is placed
inside. Gradually, the pees are attracted towards it. They make it their abode and collect
honey. The honey is extracted in Jyaistha.
On an appointed day, when night falls the
farmers burn some rags to stuff the rOom
with smoke. After this they open the lid, and
the smoke is directed towards the bee-hive.
The bees cannot stand it and run away from
the hive. The honey is then extracted with
the help of taita or a spoon. On an average,
a hive yields five to ten kacha seers of honey.
