# Project direction: Shimla, the hill states, and the Sutlej

This version narrows the 16 topics in [candidate-topics.md](candidate-topics.md) to the three
we marked (4, 9, 10) and to topics next to them. The three connect through one
mechanism. Shimla was built on land the Shimla Hill States gave up, and the
rajas of those states supplied it with porters and supplies. Most of that
traffic ran along the Sutlej valley on the Hindustan-Tibet Road. The 1948
merger removed the rajas who organised this supply. The 1961 surveys record
the villages 13 years later.

Quotes and page facts below come from `pdftotext` output of the 1961 zip in
`sources/`. OCR errors are corrected in the quotes. Line counts come from
`grep -c` and count matching lines, not occurrences.

---

## 1. The three starting ideas

**4. Merger of the princely states (1948).** Before 15 April 1948 the villages
sat in small states such as Keonthal, Bhajji, Kumharsain, Jubbal and Bushahr.
Each state had a raja, wazirs (ministers) and local revenue officials. The
merger removed all of these in one act.

**9. Shimla and its hinterland.** The Simla District Census Handbook gives the
timeline. The British kept the Shimla ridge after the Gurkha War of 1814 to
1815. Lt. Ross built a cottage there in 1819 and Lt. Kennedy built Kennedy
House in 1822. The Governor General spent the summer there in 1827. Work on the
Hindustan-Tibet Road from Kalka began in 1850, and Shimla became the formal
summer capital in 1864.

**10. The river as the unit of study.** The Sutlej valley links Shakrori, in
Bhajji State close to Shimla, to Kanum in Kinnaur, 186 miles away. The kuhl, a gravity-fed
irrigation channel cut from a stream, is the main irrigation unit in these
villages.

## 2. Related ideas

### A. Begar: forced labour between the raja, the village and Shimla

Begar is unpaid labour owed to the state or to a landholder, usually portering.
The surveys give its forms, its routes and its end.

- **Chauntra (Mandi State)** has 27 lines on begar and classifies its types.
  *Phutkar begar* covered "trifling services rendered to the State such as the
  carriage of dak" (the post). *Phant begar* and *badi jadi begar* were
  heavier grades.
- **Chergaon (Rohru)** describes the *halbandi*, the ploughman household, who
  "was supposed to do all work free of charge as desired by zaildar or
  lambardar. This was known as begar. If grains are to be carried from Rohru to
  Rampur the zaildar would depute half a dozen halbandis from each village."
- **Kanum (Kinnaur, Bushahr State)** records that dues "in cash or kind
  including 'Athada Begar'" were fixed in Sambat 1933 (about 1876 to 1877 CE)
  together with land revenue. A local caste group is still named *Bethu
  Chamang*. The survey glosses *bethu* as "servant."
- **Shakrori (Bhajji State)**: "The bethu system has been abolished."
- **Chaupal (Jubbal)**: "50 kardars (officials) and begaris (corvee labourers)
  accompany the god" on the deity's tour.

Question: how did begar work under the rajas, who owed it, where did the loads
go, and what replaced it after 1948?

### B. A capital becomes a village: Chaunri (Junga), Keonthal State

The Chaunri survey states its own purpose: to "study the changes that have
taken place as a result of its reduction from the capital of a princely state to
an ordinary village." It also records that "a part of the present town of Simla
was carved out of the territory once belonging to Keonthal State" and that
Keonthal received other land in exchange. A police training school has since
moved in. Chaunri is the one village in the corpus whose survey was written
around the merger.

The survey gives two distances to Shimla, "twelve miles" by motor road and "25
miles." We have not resolved which is correct.

### C. The Hindustan-Tibet Road as a Sutlej corridor

- **Nachar (Bushahr State)**: "The old Hindustan-Tibet mule road ... passes
  through the village." The new motor road "passes along the left bank of river
  Sutlej three miles down the village."
- **Kanum**: 5 lines on the Hindustan-Tibet Road and 34 lines on the Sutlej.
  The survey puts Kanum "186 miles away from Simla."
- **Shakrori**: on the Sutlej. The Simla-Seoni-Tatapani motor road "is one of
  the major developments after 1948."

Question: did a village's position on the road set its links to Shimla, and did
the move from mule road to motor road change which villages mattered?

### D. Shimla's electricity from the Sutlej: Chaba and Shakrori

Chaba power house stands next to Shakrori. The survey quotes Edward Buck's
*Simla Past and Present*. Shimla "was first lighted by the electricity" on 15
July 1913. The survey also says that the villagers "used to look at Chaba ...
and had never hoped that they would ever see their homes electrified." By the
survey, dated May 1963, that had happened. The survey does not give the year
Shakrori was connected. That year sets the lag between the town and the
village next to its power source.

### E. Kuhls, land classes and the state's revenue

The Shakrori survey reprints extracts from the **Bhajji State Settlement
Report, 1923**. That report rated land by irrigation class: *kiar* (irrigated),
*kulhant*, *bakhal* (unirrigated) and *tikar* (poor hill land). Each class has
a first and second grade and an estimated yield per bigha for each crop. In
1961 "one third of the area is irrigated by a kuhl." Kuhl mentions are
concentrated in Shakrori (16 lines), Kanum (15) and Chergaon (10).

Question: the raja's settlement rated land by water. Who controlled the kuhls
before 1948, and who controlled them after?

### F. Distance from Shimla as a gradient

Five surveys state a distance to Shimla.

| Village | State before 1948 | Stated distance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Chaunri | Keonthal | 12 or 25 miles |
| Batal | Baghal (Arki) | 45 km |
| Kalath | British Simla District | 45 miles |
| Shathla | Kumharsain | 52 miles |
| Kanum | Bushahr | 186 miles |

The state for Batal comes from Arki being the Baghal capital. We have not yet
checked it in the survey text. The other surveys give road links but no
distance. We can measure those from the Punjab Census Atlas or a modern map.

## 3. Research question

> **How did the growth of Shimla (1819 to 1947) draw labour, land and water
> from the Shimla Hill States along the Sutlej, and what changed in those
> villages when the 1948 merger removed the rajas who organised that supply?**

Sub-questions:

1. What did villages owe the raja and, through him, Shimla? This covers begar
   (idea A), land given up for the town (B) and water and power (D, E).
2. Did distance from Shimla, or position on the Sutlej corridor, predict how
   much a village was drawn into the town's economy by 1961? (C, F)
3. After 1948, did the old obligations end, or did they reappear in a new form,
   such as road-building wage work and jobs in Shimla? Shakrori records that
   villagers in debt to shopkeepers repaid it only "after the merger," when some
   "were able to get jobs in Simla."

Two narrower versions, if the full question is too large for 3,000 to 4,000
words:

- **Begar only.** "What was begar in the Shimla Hill States, and what replaced
  it after 1948?" Evidence from Chauntra, Chergaon, Kanum, Shakrori and
  Chaupal.
- **Two villages.** Chaunri (former capital, next to Shimla) against Shakrori
  (on the Sutlej, next to Shimla's power house). Both were tied to Shimla, one
  through land and administration, the other through water and power.

### Reading through both lenses

**Annales.** The Sutlej valley and its kuhls are the slow structure. Shimla's
growth over 1819 to 1947 is the medium-term cycle. The 1948 merger is the
event. The question asks how much the event changed a structure that the river
and the road had already fixed.

**Marxist.** Begar is unpaid labour taken by a ruling class. The raja took it
from the village and passed part of it to the colonial town. The 1923 Bhajji
settlement shows the state valuing land by its output. The question is whether
the merger freed that labour or turned it into wage labour for the same town.

## 5. Computational methods

Programming Historian (programminghistorian.org) has lessons for each step.

1. **Keyword tracing.** Run `grep` across all surveys for begar, bethu, kuhl,
   merger, Simla and road terms. Record each hit by village and section in a
   CSV. This step produced the counts above.
2. **Table extraction.** Put the Bhajji 1923 land-class yields, the 1961
   land-use tables, and the occupation tables (road work, service in Shimla)
   into spreadsheets.
3. **Mapping.** Place each village on a map with its distance to Shimla along
   the road and the river. QGIS or a simple Python plot is enough for about 12
   points.
4. **Comparison against distance.** Plot a 1961 measure, such as the share of
   households with a member working outside the village or the share of
   irrigated land, against distance from Shimla.
5. **Close reading.** Chaunri and Shakrori have the longest narrative sections
   on the merger. Read both in full.

## 6. The 1931 Census and Rose's Glossary

**1931 Census, Punjab, Vol. XVII.** The copies in `sources/` come from the
Internet Archive and are the same volumes as the JSTOR (SAOA) items. Part I
is the Report and Part II is the Tables. Part II gives separate rows for
Bashahr, Keonthal and Jubbal, and puts the smaller states, including Bhajji
and Kumharsain, under "Other Simla Hill States." With these rows we can
compare the states at the state level in 1881, 1931 and 1961. The OCR of
the table figures has errors, so each figure needs checking against the page
image before we use it.

**Rose, Glossary (1910), 3 volumes.** Lines matching for our terms:

| Term | Vol. 1 | Vol. 2 | Vol. 3 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Simla Hill(s) | 73 | 41 | 22 |
| Bashahr | 58 | 51 | 13 |
| Keonthal | 24 | 9 | 2 |
| Bhajji | 17 | 0 | 0 |
| Sutlej | 70 | 55 | 49 |
| begar | 0 | 0 | 6 |

- **Vol. 1** records the deity cults of Bhajji, the state Shakrori belonged
  to. One Bhajji god "of the low ravines" is worshipped "whenever there is a
  considerable volume of water between Arki and Suni." That is the Sutlej
  stretch around Shakrori, and it gives idea 10 a religious side.
- **Vol. 3** describes begar in Spiti households. The head of the family,
  the *khang chimpa* ("great house"), answered for "the revenue, the begar or forced labour,"
  and younger sons sometimes did "distant begar" in his place. The same
  volume records Rana landholders in Chamba who "enjoy exemption from begar."
  Together these show who owed begar and who was exempt, about 50 years before the
  1961 surveys.
