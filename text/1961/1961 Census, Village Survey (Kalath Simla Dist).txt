In the last few decades the Census has

what was happening and how fast to

and some kind of a map of the country.

CONCLUSION

Kalath is a small and backward
village of the lower Simla hills. .It is
typical of the area inasmuch as it repre'sents a community in transition with
declining dependence; on agriculture.
The residents are hard-working though
illiterate. The income of five households out of eight ranges between
Rs. 1,000 and Rs. 4,000 per year. They
cling to conventions and superstitions
rather tenaciously. Illness is attributed to the wrath of super-natural power
and the cure is supposed to lie not in
medicine but jantar-mantar, and pro,,;
pitiation of the tutelary godlings. The
sight of a pregnant woman, a beggar
with matted hair, a Rajput without his
conventional weapons and a Brahman
without tilak is regarded as an ill-omen.
On the other hand, the sight of a
sweeper or a person carrying a pitcher
of water on his head is regarded as highly auspicious. However, there are indications that the sway of these superstitions over the minds of village-folk will
decrease in the wake of new ideas. and
influences being spread by governmental agencies. Although illiteracy is so
large as to be almost universal yet all
the persons interviewed knew the name
of the Distr,ict and Panchayat headquarters and the Police-Station. Recently, a Ramdasi household has bought
a radio-set and the small village community is now exposed to the influence

of community listening programmes.
Fifty per cent of the persons interviewed knew that the village had been covered by the' Development Block, Dharampore, and seven knew the Gram Sevak.
The visits of the Grrem Sevika to this
village were not many and only two
persons seemed to know her. Four out
.of eight persons knew the Panchayat
Inspector, the Medidal Officer, the
Veterinary Officer and the Block Development Officer. The villagers were
hardly aware of the existence of the
Social Education Organiser, Agricultural Inspector and the Co-operative
Inspector and this clearly indicates that
not much effort is being made to jmprove
agriculture.

All the eight persons interviewed
were generally aware of the legislative
and executive measures extending or
restricting their grazing rights, and privileges to utilis~ forest produce. All of
them knew that forced labour had been
abolished. Six could tell about the
changes in the Hindu Succession and
the Hindu Adoption Acts. Thus, they
are generally acquainted with the measures and developments immediately
effecting their living and environment.
Given intensification of development
and welfC¥'e activities this closed village community will favourably respond
to new ideas and influences.
