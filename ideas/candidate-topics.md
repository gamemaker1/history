# Project direction ideas

The three seeds we started from — hill-station tourism, a river-valley
history, a knowledge graph — were used as *starting points* to mine the
corpus for other recurring themes, then search for real precedent on each. A
second mining pass went looking specifically for themes beyond the first
round (army service, debt, caste-status claims, founding legends, gender,
language) and turned up six more. **16 candidate historical topics** in
total, below, plus a separate toolbox of computational methods (including
the knowledge graph — a technique to apply to whichever topic is chosen, not
a topic in itself). Sources cited live in
[../text/](../text/).

**On theory:** this document reads every topic through **both** Marxist and
Annales historiography together, not as alternatives to pick between. Each
idea gives both an Annales (structural/longue-durée) reading and a Marxist
(class/material-relations) reading of the same evidence.

## Section 1 - Historical questions at a glance
 
Click through to Section 2 for the sources, precedent, and dual-lens reading behind each one.

1. **Cash crops replace subsistence farming.** Villages in Mahasu district
   stopped growing food mainly for themselves and started growing apples
   (and, in one village, even tea for export) to sell — while the government
   banned the opium some of them used to grow. Who gained and who lost when
   hill farms switched from feeding the household to feeding the market?
   → [Details](#idea-1)

2. **Trade with Tibet stops almost overnight.** Villages in Kinnaur and
   Lahaul-Spiti used to swap salt, wool, and food with Tibet across mountain
   passes by barter, no money involved. That trade collapsed almost
   overnight when the 1962 war shut the border. What happened to households
   that had built their whole livelihood around it?
   → [Details](#idea-2)

3. **The village god as landlord.** Many villages are partly run by a local
   deity (*devta*), who owns land and settles disputes through a priest or
   oracle. What does it mean that a "religious" figure is also a landlord
   and a judge — and who benefits from that arrangement?
   → [Details](#idea-3)

4. **Small kingdoms disappear overnight.** Before 1948 these villages
   belonged to tiny kingdoms — Chamba, Keonthal, Jubbal — each with its own
   king and officials. When India merged those kingdoms into Himachal
   Pradesh, that whole system of rulers and officials vanished almost
   instantly. What actually happened to the people who used to run them?
   → [Details](#idea-4)

5. **Whose forest is it?** Villagers depended on nearby forests for wood and
   grazing, but the government's Forest Department restricted how much they
   could take. How did this conflict between state control and everyday
   need actually play out, and who lost?
   → [Details](#idea-5)

6. **One village, two waves of refugees.** A single village, Tandi, took in
   two very different displaced groups within 15 years — people fleeing the
   1947 Partition, and later Tibetan refugees after 1959. Why does the same
   village keep absorbing displaced people, and what happened to them once
   they arrived?
   → [Details](#idea-6)

7. **Did land reform actually change anything?** A law in the early 1950s
   abolished the old system where a few big landlords controlled most land
   and could force people to work for free. Did that law really change who
   controlled land and labour — or did the same power just take a new name?
   → [Details](#idea-7)

8. **Tribal status, handed out one group at a time.** Between 1950 and 1966,
   the government decided — one hill community at a time — which groups
   counted as a "Scheduled Tribe" with special rights. What did it mean to
   be surveyed in 1961, mid-way through that slow rollout, still waiting to
   be classified?
   → [Details](#idea-8)

9. **Shimla grows, and the hills around it change.** Shimla went from a
   small British rest-stop in 1819 to India's summer capital, and villages
   around it slowly got roads and rest-houses built for travellers. How did
   the growth of one town reshape the work and land of villages nearby?
   → [Details](#idea-9)

10. **Let the river tell the story.** Some villages sit at key points on
    rivers — where two rivers meet, or along old irrigation channels. What
    if the river itself, not the government or the census, is treated as
    the main force shaping how people settled and farmed here?
    → [Details](#idea-10)

11. **Joining the army changed families' fortunes.** Many young men from
    certain hill castes joined the British Indian Army, and villages
    proudly record their retired soldiers and pensions. How did sending a
    son to the army change a family's status, income, and land compared to
    families that didn't?
    → [Details](#idea-11)

12. **Debt tied castes together unequally.** Village records show many
    households deep in debt to local moneylenders, sometimes at very high
    interest, with entire castes almost universally indebted. How did debt
    lock different castes into an unequal relationship with each other?
    → [Details](#idea-12)

13. **Claiming a higher caste, across 80 years of records.** Some caste
    groups in these villages started calling themselves by a
    higher-status name, like "Rajput," instead of their older caste name —
    and the very same pattern of claiming higher status shows up 80 years
    earlier in colonial records too. How and why did groups try to climb
    the caste ladder over time, and can we actually trace the same claim
    across three different-era sources?
    → [Details](#idea-13)

14. **Every village has an origin story — are they true?** Almost every
    village survey includes a story of who first settled there and where
    they came from, sometimes with an exact date centuries ago. Can these
    local legends be read as real evidence of how different groups actually
    migrated into the hills, layer by layer?
    → [Details](#idea-14)

15. **Marriage and women's status looked different here.** These hill
    villages had marriage customs unlike the plains — bride-price marriage
    instead of dowry, specific rules on widow remarriage and women's
    inheritance. How did women's social and economic position in the hills
    differ from the plains, and was it already changing by 1961?
    → [Details](#idea-15)

16. **Who got to read and write — and in what?** Some records mention an
    old script only landlords could read, or monks in Kinnaur literate in
    Tibetan script. How far was literacy itself — and literacy in a
    *particular* language or script — a form of power tied to caste or
    class?
    → [Details](#idea-16)

## Section 2 — Detailed idea descriptions

<a id="idea-1"></a>

### 1. From subsistence to cash crop: apples, tea, and the suppression of opium

**What the corpus shows:** Apple orcharding is displacing subsistence farming
across Mahasu district — `Village Survey (Chergaon - Rohru Mahasu)` describes
trucks hauling apples out of Rohru and government-distributed apple saplings;
`Village Survey (Bhrhmaur - Chamba)` and `(Batal- Arki Mahasu)` and
`(Kot - Sarkaghat Mandi)` record land being converted from grain to orchard
(apple, plum, peach, pomegranate). Stranger and more specific: `Village Survey
(Chauntra - Jogindar Nagar Mandi)` documents an actual tea factory (Raj
Kumar's) processing China/Assam varieties and exporting to Afghanistan, Tibet
and Kashmir. The same surveys' census schedules ask directly "do they have
opium habits?" and several note opium cultivation was once widespread and is
now banned — a state-driven crop substitution, not organic decline.

**Precedent:** the apple economy's own historiography is well developed —
"Horticultural Pathways to Economic Transformation: Apple Cultivation in
Shimla District, Himachal Pradesh" examines the class differentiation (land,
capital, market access) that orcharding produced; "Apple Growers' Associations
in Northwestern India" (*Human Organization* 67[1]) covers the institutional/
political economy of grower associations; "Apple Economy and Social
Development in Himachal Pradesh" traces the longue-durée arc from missionary
Samuel (Satyanand) Stokes's introduction of the crop to present-day prosperity
and its paradoxes.

**Annales layer:** a slow structural shift — subsistence grain agriculture
giving way, district by district, to commercial monoculture over decades,
visible mid-transition in 1961.

**Marxist layer:** who captures the new capital (orchard-owning households,
Raj Kumar's factory) versus who is squeezed out (opium growers banned
outright, tenants without capital to convert land) — cash-cropping as a
redistribution of who controls value, not just a change of crop.

<a id="idea-2"></a>

### 2. Trans-Himalayan trade and its rupture

**What the corpus shows:** Kinnaur and Lahaul-Spiti villages sit directly on
old Tibet trade routes. `Village Survey (Nachar - Kinnaur)` and `(Kanum -
Kinnaur)` describe mule caravans on "the old Hindustan-Tibet mule road" (by
1961 partly motorable); Kanum records villagers historically bringing "butter,
ghee, goat, sheep and salt from Tibet" — direct barter-trade evidence;
`(Basal- Solon Mahasu)` even notes a "common boundary with Tibet." The Lavi
fair goods recorded in `(Shathla - Kumarsain Mahasu)` and `(Pangna - Karsog
Mandi)` (Gudmas, Namdas, pashmina) are the wool/pashmina end of the same trade
network, and `(Chaupal - Mahasu)` describes dedicated mule-caravan halting
places already being displaced by motor traffic in 1961.

**Precedent:** "The commodity exchange between Western Tibet and Kinnaur:
symbolic geographies and the Himalayan cross-border trade" (*GeoJournal*)
analyzes the *gamgya* oath-based salt-grain barter system and its rupture after
the 1962 India-China war closed the border/Shipki La pass; "Indo-Tibetan Wool
Politics" (*Himalayan Journal*) covers the wool trade's political economy and
disruption; a SAGE preprint on trans-Himalayan trade routes covers the
Hindustan-Tibet Road and the Rampur Bushahr Lavi Fair directly.

**Annales layer:** a centuries-old trade structure (barter, oath-based
contracts, caravan geography) captured by the census just before its 1962
rupture — a rare case where we can date the end of a longue-durée structure
almost to the year.

**Marxist layer:** who controlled the terms of exchange (barter rates, caravan
labour, middlemen at fairs like Lavi) before the rupture, and what replaced
that trade-based livelihood afterward for households that had depended on it.

<a id="idea-3"></a>

### 3. Devta governance and the temple-land economy

**What the corpus shows:** Himachal's local deity (*devta*) institutions
appear as functioning landholding, dispute-resolving polities, not just
religious color. `Village Survey (Devi-Kothi, Chamba)` details temple land
distributed among functionaries (Batwals etc.) and flags that its tenure was
"likely to undergo" change post-merger; `(Kothi - Kinnaur)` records ~12 acres
of temple land let out on *batai* (sharecropping); `(Chergaon - Rohru Mahasu)`
has the richest anecdote in the whole corpus — Jabal-ka-devta's oracle settles
livestock-theft disputes by oath, once refused to enter a Rajput's house
(causing "a fuss"), and in Sondha village a rule holds that the devta's
ritual path must never run below the level of Harijan houses — caste
hierarchy encoded directly into sacred geography.

**Precedent:** Chetan Singh, "Constructing the State in the Western Himalaya"
(*Journal of Punjab Studies*, 2013) argues Himalayan ruling elites built
legitimacy by integrating village devta cults into a state-level hierarchy of
deities — religion as political-economic order, not backdrop. William S. Sax,
*In the Valley of the Kauravas: A Divine Kingdom in the Western Himalaya*
(OUP, 2024) and "At the Center of Power: Ritual Assemblies and the Making of
Gods and Kings in the Western Himalaya" (*Intl Journal of Hindu Studies*,
2025) both treat deity/king power-sharing as a real historical institution.

**Annales layer:** the devta polity as a structure that survives the
colonial-to-postcolonial transition largely intact, changing only slowly
(temple land tenure "likely to undergo" change, not undergoing it yet in
1961).

**Marxist layer:** temple land as a landholding institution with its own
tenants and sharecroppers (batai) — the devta polity is also a landlord, and
the caste-coded ritual-path rule is a material hierarchy as much as a
religious one.

<a id="idea-4"></a>

### 4. Princely-state merger (1948) as administrative and social rupture

**What the corpus shows:** `Village Survey (Chaunri - Kasumpti Mahasu)` gives
a full legendary/political history of Keonthal State — a succession dispute
between Sirmur and Nalagarh, a Guhel Rajput enthroned via a blood-tilak ritual
still ritually reenacted — and explicitly narrates the village's "reduction
from the capital of a princely state to an ordinary village" after merger.
`(Bhrhmaur - Chamba)` details Chamba State's Wazir/Amin/Mahta/Kotwal/Palasra
administrative hierarchy (with separate Wazirs for Pangi and Brahmaur) and how
temple administration changed "before/after merger." `(Batal- Arki Mahasu)`
and `(Chaupal - Mahasu)` narrate Jubbal State's Wazir Sher Singh and a fort
burnt down in his time, later replaced by a temple.

**Precedent:** Wikipedia's "History of Himachal Pradesh," "Bilaspur State,"
and "Chamba State" give the documentary skeleton — the 15 April 1948 merger of
Chamba, Mandi, Suket, Sirmaur and ~26 smaller Shimla Hill States, with
Bilaspur merging separately in 1954. No dedicated academic study of what
happened to individual jagirdars/wazirs after 1948 was found — this is a real
gap in the literature that a close reading of these village surveys (which
each independently narrate their own state's dissolution) could actually
help fill, not just illustrate.

**Annales layer:** merger as a sudden administrative "event" imposed on a much
slower structure of local dynastic legitimacy (the blood-tilak ritual
persisting in memory/practice even after the state itself is gone).

**Marxist layer:** merger as a redistribution of who controls land and
authority — what happened materially to wazirs, jagirdars, and temple
administrators when the state apparatus that employed and legitimated them
disappeared.

<a id="idea-5"></a>

### 5. Forest rights: state forestry versus subsistence use

**What the corpus shows:** Deodar, kail, fir and chil are named commercial
timber species with explicit Forest Department royalty rates in `Bhrhmaur`,
`Chaunri`, `Moginand`, and `(Kot - Sarkaghat Mandi)`. Grazing-rights
restriction is a standing census-schedule question, answered in `Bhrhmaur`,
`Shathla`, and `Kalath`. `(Basal- Solon Mahasu)` records Kolis explicitly
losing their forest rights; `(Maingal - Chamba)` records villagers openly
critical of Forest Department officials over management — a live
extraction-vs-subsistence conflict, not settled history.

**Precedent:** "Colonial and Post-Colonial Forest Governance: Environmental
Change and Contested Commons in Chamba, Himachal Pradesh" directly fills the
Guha/Kumaon-style gap for Chamba specifically — exactly matching several of
our Chamba-district surveys. "The Mirage of Permanent Boundaries: Politics of
Forest Reservation" compares Kumaon/Garhwal/Punjab Hill States forest-boundary
politics. Richard P. Tucker's *A Forest History of India* gives the standard
reference chapters on Himalayan deodar extraction.

**Annales layer:** the forest as a slow-changing ecological structure whose
administrative *control* (colonial reservation, royalty schedules) is
imposed on top of it as a series of policy events.

**Marxist layer:** the Koli case is the sharpest possible material — a caste
group losing a subsistence right (forest access) to state/commercial timber
extraction, a direct dispossession narrative sitting inside otherwise dry
census prose.

<a id="idea-6"></a>

### 6. Double displacement at Tandi: Partition refugees and Tibetan refugees in one village

**What the corpus shows:** `Village Survey (Tandi Lahaul-Spiti)` has a
dedicated section on Khampas (Tibetan refugees) and a census schedule that
directly asks about "area vacated by the out-going [population]" and the
"effect of the incoming of refugees on language, dress and general mode of
life" — layering two distinct 1940s-1960s displacement waves (1947 Partition,
then post-1959 Tibetan refugee influx) onto the same small village.
`(Basal- Solon Mahasu)` separately records the Bhatra caste as having "come
from Sialkot in Pakistan after the partition" — a named, traceable Partition
migration into a specific Himachal village, distinct from the much more
studied Punjab-plains Partition literature.

**Precedent:** genuinely thin — no dedicated Himachal-specific Partition
resettlement scholarship was found (Himachal appears in the literature mostly
as a transit/relief-camp hub via Shimla, not a resettlement destination); the
nearest work is general Punjab-plains rehabilitation historiography (e.g.
"Riots, Refugees and Rehabilitation: A Case Study of Punjab 1946-56"). That
gap is itself the pitch: a close reading of Tandi's own census answers could
be a small but real original contribution rather than an application of
existing work.

**Annales layer:** Tandi as a structural "receiving node" for displacement,
twice in fifteen years, independent of who specifically is displaced each
time — geography (a Lahaul-Spiti confluence village on a through-route) as
destiny.

**Marxist layer:** what economic role each refugee group is slotted into on
arrival (the Bhatra caste's occupation at Basal; whatever livelihood the
Khampas take up at Tandi) — displacement as a redistribution of labour, not
just of population.

<a id="idea-7"></a>

### 7. Zamindari abolition and the 1953 Land Reforms Act as agrarian class transition

**What the corpus shows:** `Village Survey (Bhrhmaur - Chamba)` explicitly
logs land-tenure change under the Himachal Pradesh Abolition of Zamindari and
Big Landed Estates Act, including the abolition of begar (forced labour) for
temple priests. This sits inside a corpus where economy sections throughout
detail landholding size, tenancy, and wage labour just after this Act and just
before further land reform.

**Precedent:** Kusum Verma's study of the Act's tenancy-security and
resumption-ceiling provisions, and early litigation like *Shree Vinod Kumar &
Others vs State of Himachal Pradesh* (1958), give the legal-historical
skeleton. No explicitly Marxist-framed academic treatment of *this specific
Act* was found — general Indian zamindari-abolition Marxist historiography
(Daniel Thorner, Utsa Patnaik) is the adjacent framework to apply, again a
gap our own village-level evidence could help fill rather than just cite.

**Annales layer:** the Act as a sharp "event" cutting across the much slower
structure of who-farms-whose-land that the village surveys otherwise describe
as continuous.

**Marxist layer:** the direct question — did the Act actually redistribute
control of land and labour (begar's abolition is a real, named case), or did
it just rename the same relations? This is the most directly Marxist-legible
material in the whole corpus.

<a id="idea-8"></a>

### 8. Scheduled Tribe status as a sequential political-legal process

**What the corpus shows:** the demographic tables in `Report on Sc and St of
Himachal Pradesh` and the Special Tables volume are just that — tables, with
no debate language recoverable by direct search. But the *fact* of sequential
notification is itself the story, and several village surveys (Gaddi
material in the SC/ST report, Kinnaura material in the Kinnaur surveys,
Lahaula material in Tandi) sit exactly on the fault lines of that timeline.

**Precedent:** a Shodhganga thesis chapter, "Scheduled Areas and Scheduled
Tribes of Himachal Pradesh," gives the actual notification timeline: Gaddi
(1950), Kinnaura/Gujjar/Jad (1951–56), Lamba/Pangwala/Lahaula (1966) — meaning
Lahaula and Pangwal villages in this very corpus (Tandi, the Pangi surveys)
were surveyed in 1961 *before* their own ST status existed yet. "Scheduled
Tribes of Himachal Pradesh: An Ethno-geographical Perspective" (*Himalayan
Journal*) gives the ethno-geographic framing.

**Annales layer:** classification as a slow-moving administrative structure
extending itself group by group over sixteen years (1950–1966), with our 1961
census sitting mid-sequence — a structure caught visibly incomplete.

**Marxist layer:** ST status carries material entitlements (land, quotas); the
sequencing of who got classified when is a direct allocation of future
material advantage, not a neutral ethnographic tidying-up.

<a id="idea-9"></a>

### 9. Hill-station tourism development (Shimla) and its village hinterland

**What the corpus shows:** `District Census Handbook - Simla` narrates
Shimla's 1819 origin as a sanatorium, Kennedy House (1822), its 1827
elevation to summer capital, and the 1850 Hindustan-Tibet Road out of Kalka.
`(Chergaon - Rohru Mahasu)` explicitly anticipates tourism once a planned road
arrives, plus an existing trout farm. Nearly every survey logs a Forest/PWD
Rest House — the same colonial leisure-infrastructure logic as Shimla,
radiating outward at smaller scale.

**Precedent:** Pamela Kanwar, *Imperial Simla*; Judith Kenny, "Climate, Race,
and Imperial Authority" (*Annals of the AAG*, 1995); Dane Kennedy, *The Magic
Mountains*; the Darjeeling leisure-economy/land-labour study as a comparative
template tying hill-station tourism directly to surrounding land and labour.

**Annales layer:** the longue-durée arc from 1819 sanatorium to 1961's
still-expanding rest-house network to present-day overtourism.

**Marxist layer:** labour drawn into the hill-station economy (rest-house
staff, forest coolies, begar) versus subsistence villages nearby.

<a id="idea-10"></a>

### 10. The river/watershed as the unit of analysis

**What the corpus shows:** `(Tandi Lahaul-Spiti)` sits exactly at the
Chandra-Bhaga confluence forming the Chandrabhaga/Chenab, with a bridge, a
Manali road, and ritual bathing. `Report on Sc and St of Himachal Pradesh`
(Newell's Gaddi monograph) frames the Ravi valley and Budhal Nadi as the
ecological-political unit of Brahmaur and Gaddi transhumance. Several Mandi
surveys mention kuhls (gravity irrigation channels) off the Sutlej-Beas
watershed as a working communal institution.

**Precedent:** Chetan Singh, *Natural Premises: Ecology and Peasant Life in
the Western Himalaya, 1800–1950* — the closest direct Annales-style match for
this exact region. Ramachandra Guha, *The Unquiet Woods*, as the Kumaon
parallel. No HP-specific river-valley-as-unit historiography beyond Singh was
found.

**Annales layer:** the river/watershed as the slowest possible structure,
against which administrative and demographic change is mere "event."

**Marxist layer:** the kuhl as a communal-labour institution — who
builds/controls it, and whether that shifts with zamindari abolition.

*(Overlaps with #2 in Kinnaur/Lahaul-Spiti — a combined trade-route-as-river-
corridor framing is possible if the group wants one topic instead of two.)*

<a id="idea-11"></a>

### 11. Army recruitment and the status economy of military service

**What the corpus shows:** `(Kot - Sarkaghat Mandi)` records Brahmins/Swarans
"serving in the army," a photo caption of "retired armymen proudly
displaying..." and mentions of military pensioners. `(Chergaon - Rohru
Mahasu)`'s occupation table lists Rajputs' second occupation as "Military
Service." `(Bhrhmaur - Chamba)` names army service "outside" the village as a
factor of change. `(Nachar - Kinnaur)` names a specific ex-serviceman.
`(Devi-Kothi, Chamba)`'s census schedule itself asks "Is there any
ex-serviceman living [in the village]?" — the census tracked this as a
category in its own right — and the *District Handbook, Mahasu* has a
dedicated table on ex-servicemen and the relief provided to them.

**Precedent:** Tan Tai Yong, *The Garrison State: The Military, Government
and Society in Colonial Punjab, 1849–1947* — shows "martial race" recruitment
was concentrated in specific hill/submontane military districts (Kangra,
Chamba-adjacent tracts), not Punjab generally. Dogra Regiment recruiting
history documents recruiting fairs (melas) run directly in Himachal-hill
territory. "Army Recruitment and Patron-Client Relationship in Colonial
Punjab: A Grassroots Perspective" (*Pakistan Vision*, 2015) covers how local
landed patrons brokered recruitment — a dynamic this corpus's Rajput/Brahmin
recruitment pattern fits closely.

**Annales layer:** military service as a long-standing structural pathway
for specific castes (Rajput, Swaran) that persists in near-identical form
from the colonial army into the postcolonial one, tracked by the census as
its own stable category (ex-servicemen, pensions) rather than a passing event.

**Marxist layer:** army pay and pensions as a source of capital outside the
village land economy entirely — did soldiering let some households buy land
or status that agriculture alone couldn't, and did that create a visible gap
between military and non-military households of the same caste?

<a id="idea-12"></a>

### 12. Debt and moneylending: caste-stratified credit relations

**What the corpus shows:** `(Kalath - Simla)` has detailed indebtedness
tables naming a specific bania moneylender ("Bhagwan Dass...Bania
Bhojnagar"), land mortgaged to named Banias, and a household borrowing
"Rs 1500 in 1960 from money-lenders" with the interest rate discussed.
`(Pangna - Karsog Mandi)` records "73 out of 107 households are under debt,"
totalling "37,415" owed to "village money-lenders." `(Maingal - Chamba)`
records Gujjar households "cent per cent under debt" at "30% annual"
interest from Chamba moneylenders, and separately Gaddi grain-debts owed "to
Gaddi brahmins" — debt running along caste lines in more than one direction
at once.

**Precedent:** Malcolm Lyall Darling, *The Punjab Peasant in Prosperity and
Debt* (1925) — the classic study of Punjab peasant indebtedness, explicitly
comparing submontane hill districts (Kangra, Hoshiarpur) to the plains; Clive
Dewey's 1985 *Journal of Peasant Studies* reassessment of Darling's data and
method is useful for methodological caution if this angle is pursued
quantitatively.

**Annales layer:** indebtedness as a persistent structural relation between
specific caste-occupational roles (bania lender, cultivator/herder borrower)
that outlasts individual loans — a durable credit hierarchy, not a series of
one-off transactions.

**Marxist layer:** the sharpest material in the whole corpus for a direct
class argument — named interest rates, named lenders, whole castes
("cent per cent") under debt to another caste. This is credit as a
mechanism of extraction, quantifiable from the sources themselves.

<a id="idea-13"></a>

### 13. Caste status mobility: "returning themselves as" Rajput across three sources

**What the corpus shows:** `(Chaunri - Kasumpti Mahasu)` and `(Moginand,
Sirmur)` use near-identical phrasing — lower-status groups "have returned
themselves as" Rajput and "are reluctant to be called" their original caste
name. `(Chaupal - Mahasu)` records a group that "claim a higher status."
These are 1961 census-taker observations of caste-status claims *in
progress*. H.A. Rose's 1910 *Glossary* (Vol. 3) independently documents
dozens of groups "claiming Rajput descent" from the 1881 census era — the
same claim-making pattern, 80 years earlier, in a completely different
source.

**Precedent:** Jonathan P. Parry, *Caste and Kinship in Kangra* (1979) — a
full ethnographic-historical account of Rajput hypergamy and status-mobility
processes among Punjab-hill castes, engaging directly with the colonial
categories (Ibbetson/Rose) this corpus also uses. The general concept is
M.N. Srinivas's "Sanskritization," though no source found applies that term
to Himachal specifically — Parry's Kangra study is the more precise regional
match.

**Annales layer:** caste-status claiming as a slow, recurring structural
process, not a one-off event — the same kind of claim (lower caste →
"Rajput") recorded independently in 1881-era Rose material and again in the
1961 surveys, suggesting a durable status-mobility mechanism spanning the
colonial/postcolonial divide.

**Marxist layer:** claiming Rajput status isn't just symbolic — it plausibly
tracks and enables real material gains (marriage alliances, land, access to
military service per #11) — a caste claim as a material strategy, not merely
an identity choice.

*(This is the idea best suited to actually using all three source types
together — 1881 Ibbetson, 1910 Rose, 1961 surveys — on the very same
question, since the same claim-language shows up independently on both
ends.)*

<a id="idea-14"></a>

### 14. Village founding and migration legends

**What the corpus shows:** `(Rajana - Rainka, Sirmur)` records "the Rajputs
were the first settlers who came from..." and a named ancestor ("one Puja
came from Delhi... hundred years ago"). `(Basal - Solon Mahasu)` gives a
layered settlement sequence — original settlers, then Brahmins, then Bhatras
"from Sialkot," then blacksmiths also from Sialkot. `(Maingal - Chamba)`
traces Gujjar migration "from Gujar Des" and a separate Rajput migration
story from Gujarat. `(Pangna - Karsog)` dates its founding precisely: "Bir
Sen first settled down at [Pangna] year 778 A.D." `(Chauntra - Jogindar
Nagar, Mandi)` traces Brahmin/Rajput migration "from Lad-Bharol illaqa."

**Precedent:** Mahesh Sharma, "State Formation and Cultural Complex in
Western Himalaya: Chamba Genealogy and Epigraphs, 700–1650 CE" (*IESHR*,
2004) treats exactly this kind of clan genealogy as real historical evidence
of settlement/state formation. Chetan Singh, "Constructing the State in the
Western Himalaya" (2013) covers how migration-linked clan cults were
integrated into hill-state structures. The broader Khasa-migration theory
(ancestors of today's Kanets in Himachal, and related groups across
Kumaon/Garhwal/Nepal) is the standard comparative frame if a
Himalaya-wide angle is wanted instead of a Himachal-only one.

**Annales layer:** these legends, read across several villages together, sketch
successive migration "waves" into the same hill tracts over centuries — a
settlement structure visible only by comparing many villages' stories side by
side, not from any one alone.

**Marxist layer:** migration order maps suspiciously well onto later social
rank in several of these villages (the "first settlers" claim is
itself often a status claim) — settlement legend as a materially useful
story to be first, not just a memory.

<a id="idea-15"></a>

### 15. Gender, marriage payment (reet), and women's status

**What the corpus shows:** `(Pangna - Karsog)` has a dedicated "Reet
Marriage" section describing bride-price/elopement marriage in detail.
`(Shathla - Kumarsain)` gives an explicit sex ratio ("908 females per 1,000
males") attributed partly to male out-migration for service, alongside a
statement that "a woman enjoys equal [status]." `(Kanum - Kinnaur)` records a
widow given "Rs. 500... as izzat" and has a dedicated "Widow Remarriage"
section. Several villages (`Bir - Mandi`, `Chitrari`, `Kothi - Kinnaur`,
`Bhrhmaur`) carry a standardized census-schedule question, "Is widow
remarriage permitted in the community?", alongside dedicated "Status of
Women" sections — directly comparable across villages.

**Precedent:** an article in the *International Journal of Historical
Insight and Research*, "Various Forms of Matrimony in Himachal Pradesh,"
examines reet, jhanjhara, karewa, and polyandry across c.1890s–1920s
Himachal and the shift from bride-price toward dowry under colonial/reform
pressure. Jonathan P. Parry's *Caste and Kinship in Kangra* (1979) also
covers marriage payment and hypergamy among the same hill castes. A useful
later bookend: the 2015 Himachal Pradesh High Court ruling extending Hindu
Succession Act inheritance rights to tribal women in Kinnaur/Lahaul-Spiti/
Chamba — a clean colonial-to-postcolonial endpoint for a longue-durée
argument.

**Annales layer:** reet and widow-remarriage customs as a durable regional
marriage-system structure, distinct from and older than the plains dowry
system, still intact but already being asked about explicitly (as if under
observation/pressure) by the 1961 census schedule itself.

**Marxist layer:** marriage payment is a direct transfer of value between
households — reet functions economically, not just socially, and the
widow's Rs. 500 "izzat" payment at Kanum is a quantifiable instance of that.

<a id="idea-16"></a>

### 16. Language, script, and literacy as status markers

**What the corpus shows:** `(Chergaon - Rohru Mahasu)`'s local glossary
records "Tankara — an old script used by landlords," a literacy marker
specific to the landholding caste. `(Kanum - Kinnaur)` documents Tibetan
script literacy at a monastery in detail, with a literacy table explicitly
splitting those who can "write Tibetan scriptures" from others. `(Nachar -
Kinnaur)` and `(Kothi - Kinnaur)` distinguish the local Kinnauri dialect from
Pahari, including a local place-name ("Nalche") differing from the official
one ("Nachar"). Also notable: the same Chergaon glossary records "Nagdi
Lagar — a form of mortgage deed," a named indigenous legal instrument
connecting this idea directly to #12's debt material.

**Precedent:** no dedicated search was run for this theme specifically, so
treat the historical framing as building directly from the primary evidence
rather than a ready-made secondary literature — general sociolinguistics of
script-as-class-marker (e.g. literacy in Persian/Urdu as an administrative-
class marker in colonial North India) is the nearest transferable frame if
one is wanted, but this idea currently rests more on close reading than on
citation.

**Annales layer:** literacy in a specific script (Tankara, Tibetan) as a
slow-changing marker of who has historically held administrative or
religious authority, largely unconnected to the census-year "events"
otherwise dominating these surveys.

**Marxist layer:** if only landlords could read Tankara, literacy itself was
restricted along class lines — a concrete case of knowledge/information
access as a form of property.

## Computational methods toolbox

Pick a method to serve the chosen topic above — not the reverse. Knowledge
graphs belong here as one option, not as a project of their own. Organized by
pipeline stage: (A) getting clean structured data out of the sources, (B)
turning that into linkable/analyzable data, (C) the actual downstream
analysis. Each entry says whether it's the "classic" approach we already have
working, or a genuinely current (2023–2026) upgrade — and flags plainly where
the upgrade would be overkill at this corpus's scale (~40 documents).

### A. Extraction

- **Baseline (already running):** `pdftotext`/Tesseract OCR + `pandoc` for the
  one EPUB — see `scripts/extract_all.sh`. This turned out sufficient for
  nearly the whole corpus, since the sources arrived pre-OCR'd.
- **LLM-based structured extraction (upgrade).** Instead of writing regex
  against messy OCR'd tables, prompt an LLM (OpenAI structured outputs /
  JSON schema, Anthropic tool use, or Gemini structured output) to pull a
  table (e.g. household/caste/occupation columns) directly out of a page of
  raw extracted text into JSON — it generalizes across the surveys'
  inconsistent table formatting and can correct OCR noise in the same pass.
  Real DH precedent: Cambridge's *Computational Humanities Research*,
  "Retrieving information from unstructured historical sources using large
  language models" (2024). Genuinely fits this project's scale — no
  fine-tuning, just an API key and a schema; Simon Willison's `llm` CLI has a
  ready-made schema mode for quick scripting.
- **Layout-aware VLM OCR (upgrade, only relevant to the handful of scanned/
  EPUB sources).** For anything Tesseract handles badly (tables interleaved
  with prose), feed the page image directly to a vision-capable LLM
  (GPT-4o/Claude/Gemini) and ask for Markdown+table output, or use an
  open-source document-VLM like Allen AI's **olmOCR** (explicitly benchmarked
  on old scans) or GOT-OCR2.0. Since our corpus turned out almost entirely
  native-text already, full local VLM self-hosting (olmOCR) is more machinery
  than needed — the vision-LLM-on-a-page-image shortcut is the
  scale-appropriate version of this.

### B. Processing

- **Structured table extraction → CSV.** Pull population/caste/occupation/
  land-use tables (by hand, or via the LLM method in A) into spreadsheets for
  direct quantitative comparison — best fit for #1, #8, #9, #11 and #12
  (anything needing a gradient or timeline across villages/years).
- **Historical NER + place-name entity linking (upgrade).** Instead of
  manually spotting every person/caste/clan/place mention, run a transformer
  NER pipeline (spaCy's `en_core_web_trf`, or `spacy-llm` for zero-shot
  extraction with an LLM as the backend) over the corpus, then resolve place
  names to a gazetteer — the **World Historical Gazetteer** (has a
  reconciliation API and stores attested historical spelling variants) or the
  **Getty Thesaurus of Geographic Names** — to handle colonial-era spelling
  drift (Simla/Shimla, and similar variants across the 1881/1961 sources).
  Directly useful for #10's river/corridor mapping and for cross-referencing
  place names between the 1881 and 1961 material.
- **LLM-assisted knowledge graph construction (upgrade to the manual
  hand-coding below).** Rather than hand-coding every entity/relation,
  LangChain's `LLMGraphTransformer`, Neo4j's no-code LLM Knowledge Graph
  Builder, or `spacy-llm` can extract nodes/edges from raw passages
  automatically, output straight into networkx/Gephi. Realistic at this
  scale; Microsoft's fuller GraphRAG (community-summary layer) is more than
  this corpus needs — stick to the plain graph-transformer output.
- **Knowledge graph / network analysis (the graph itself, classic or
  LLM-assisted).** Entities (person, clan, hamlet, caste, land-parcel, deity)
  and relations (kin, marriage, tenancy, labour-obligation, trade-partner,
  debt) from the richest 2–3 surveys for the chosen topic, built either by
  hand or via the LLM-assisted extraction above, then read structurally
  (centrality, clustering) as supporting evidence. Fits especially well with
  #3 (devta/temple land), #4 (princely administrative hierarchy), #7
  (tenancy/labour relations), #12 (debt networks), and #13 (caste-claim
  chains across sources). No India-specific census-to-graph project was
  found in precedent search, so this stays a genuine application of the
  method either way.

### C. Downstream analysis

- **Keyword/thematic search across the corpus** (what produced this very
  list) — scalable once all files are in `data/extracted_text/`; good for
  tracing one theme (e.g. mela mentions, forest royalty rates, "returned
  themselves as") across every village survey at once. Still the right
  first move before reaching for anything below.
- **Semantic search + topic clustering (upgrade).** Embed each
  document/paragraph with sentence-transformers (`all-MiniLM-L6-v2` runs
  fine on CPU for a ~40-document corpus) or a hosted embedding API, then
  cluster with **BERTopic** (embeddings → UMAP → HDBSCAN → topic terms).
  Finds thematically related passages even when vocabulary differs across
  census years — an advance over plain grep for tracing how a theme (e.g.
  classification language, land-tenure vocabulary, caste-status claims) shifts
  between the 1881, 1910, and 1961 documents — directly useful for #13.
- **Retrieval-augmented question-answering over the corpus (navigation aid,
  not analysis).** Upload the extracted-text corpus to **Google NotebookLM**
  (free, zero setup) or a minimal LlamaIndex/LangChain RAG pipeline to ask
  "what did the surveys say about X" and get cited passages back instantly,
  instead of manually rereading 40 documents. Useful for Stage 1
  scoping/comparison-table building (`Project_Plan.md` §2) — but the
  citations it returns are a starting point for close reading, not a
  substitute for the historical argument itself.
- **Text-reuse / template-diffing (upgrade to manual diffing).** The 23
  village surveys share a colonial-into-postcolonial administrative template;
  finding where they diverge from it is itself a small finding. Dedicated
  tools exist (TRACER, from the eTRAP text-reuse research group; BLAST-style
  sequence alignment adapted for OCR-noisy text) but are more setup than this
  corpus needs — the same sentence-embedding similarity approach from the
  point above, run pairwise across the 23 surveys, is enough to flag
  near-identical template sentences versus genuinely village-specific prose.
- **Close reading / micro-history.** For #4, #6, #14, and the devta anecdotes
  in #3, the single richest move may just be careful reading of one or two
  village surveys' own narrative sections — not every idea needs heavy
  computation to satisfy the brief; the announcement wants the method to
  emerge from the source, and some sources are already dense enough on their
  own.
- **Simple mapping.** Plot villages against the Punjab Census Atlas or a
  basic distance/route sketch — useful for #2, #9, #10 (anything organized
  around distance from a node or position along a corridor).
- **Graph neural networks — deliberately not recommended.** For a graph of
  dozens of nodes (the realistic scale here), classic centrality/community
  detection in Gephi or networkx already gets full interpretive value; GNNs
  need far more data to outperform them and would add complexity without
  adding insight. Worth stating this explicitly in the methodology write-up
  as a considered choice, not an omission.

## Recommendation

Strongest candidates, in rough order of how well-supported and original they
are: **#12 (debt/moneylending)** and **#7 (zamindari abolition)** are the
most directly Marxist-legible material, with named interest rates and
quantifiable indebtedness ready to analyze. **#13 (caste status mobility)**
is the most structurally interesting for this specific corpus, since it's
the one idea that can actually use all three source periods (1881, 1910,
1961) on the same question, rather than treating them as separate context.
**#3 (devta governance)** has the richest and strangest primary evidence
(the oracle/caste-path anecdote) with solid secondary literature. Whichever
is chosen, apply both lenses together (see `Project_Plan.md` §2), and pick a
method from the toolbox above to fit it — not the reverse.
