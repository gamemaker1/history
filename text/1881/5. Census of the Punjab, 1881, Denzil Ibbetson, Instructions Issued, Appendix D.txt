Preliminary.

No. 7, dated Simla, 27th August 1SS0.
From—W. M. YOUNG, Esq., Secretary to Government, Punjab,
To—All Heads of Departments, Commissioners, Deputy Commissioners, and Presidents of Municipal and Cantonment
Committees in the Punjab.
I am directed to inform you that the Government of India has decided that a census of the whole of India shall be
taken on some one night in February 1881 which will be fixed hereafter. Each district will be divided into convenient
sections for the purposes of enumeration and supervision ; and the subsequent operations prior to compilation will
include—(1) the formation of a list of houses and families, which will serve as a check-register and index; (2) a
preliminary record of census entries, to be made shortly before the night fixed for the final census ; (3) the final census,
at which the preliminary record will be brought into agreement with the facts as they stand on the night of the census.
2. The first two of these operations will be conducted more or less at leisure, but the third operation must be performed over the whole province within a single night, [t is manifest that the correctness of the final returns will
depend in great measure upon the care with which the list 0 Ihouses and families, and the preliminary record of census
entries, are prepared, and upon the capability of the staff employed for making the final entries to revise the preliminary
returns in accordance with actual facts. The staff will have to be trained and practised most carefully before the night
of the census, to ensure their effecting with accuracy the final revision of the preliminary papers. This will have to be
done by frequent rehearsals, by means of which the aptitude of the enumerators to take the final census should be
thoroughly tested before the time comes for the final operation. The accuracy of the final returns will depend mainh
uuon the supervision exercised over the formation of the preliminary registers and records and over the experimental
rehearsals.
3 . The determination of the supervising agency is one of the most important questions connected with the subject.
Every available officer in every department of Government who can be relied upon for efficiently conducting this supervision will be expected to render assistance ; the whole of the district staff in the Revenue and Judicial Departments,
the officers of the Police of and above the rank of sergeant, the superior officers of the Public Works, Educational,
Medical, Forest, Registration and other departments of Government, should be freely utilised for this important dutv ;
and heads of departments are requested to place the services of all such officers unreservedly at the disposal of tiie
district officers for this purpose, so far as may be compatible with the conduct of necessary business. The ranks of the
enumerators will likewise be filled by capable persons, who can be supplemented from all departments of Government;
only the rank and file of the Police are not to be employed in enumeration. It is anticipated that no difficulty will be
experienced in performing both the supervision and enumeration by means of unpaid agency. The occasion is one nn
which the district officers may reasonably expect to be assisted by all well-wishers of the Government, whether enjoying
any rank or privileges at the hands of the Government or not; and, in accordance with this principle, it is expected that
all honorary magistrates, jagirdars, members of municipal committees, rural notables, European and Native gentry, as
well as salaried officers of Government, will readily render such assistance as they are able in carrying out the operations
of the census. It will probably be convenient that some gazetted officer of Government should be placed in charge of
each tahsfl or local sub-division. Next to him will come the superintendents and supervisors taken from the superior
ranks of the Government service, with the addition of such unpaid agency as may be available for this employment.
Finally, the enumerators, each in charge of a block which can conveniently be traversed within the time available for the
final revision. These will mainly consist of subordinate officers of Government, supplemented also bv unpaid agencv.
The whole of this staff should be appointed at an early date, and should proceed to the compilation of the preliminarv
registers and records ; after which they should be practised in their several duties by frequent rehearsals and experimental
enumerations. Further details regarding the size of the supervisors' and enumerators' circles, and the precise modern which the rehearsals should be conducted, are left to the special officers charged with the conduct of the provincial
census. The Deputy Commissioner should lose no time in explaining thoroughly to all whose services may be required the great importance of the subject, and in determining the persons by whom every branch of the operations will
be conducted.
4. It is evident that, with the vast population we have to deal with, many of the actual enumerators will be not of
a high type of intelligence. I am therefore desired to impress upon all district officers the absolute necessity of the most
active and incessant supervision over the whole of the proceedings. Where the superior officers are constantly moving
about the district, inspecting, instructing, explaining and encouraging, the census will be well done; where they are not,
it will be done badly. The Lieutenant-Governor is fully aware that a very considerable amount of extra labour will be
imposed upon all concerned in census operations. But these operations recur only after long intervals ; the}' form part
of a scheme extending over the whole of the British Empire ; and it is all-important that no effort should be spared to
ensure their successful performance. His Honor confidently reckons upon the hearty efforts of all officers to secure this
end ; and he desires that, so far as is necessary to it, and especially at the taking of the final census, census duties should
be considered paramount.
5. Mr. Denzil Ibbetson, C.S., has been appointed Deputy Superintendent of Census Operations for the Punjab, in
direct subordination to the Local Government, and, eventually, to the Imperial Census Commissioner ; and he will
correspond with you direct on all matters falling within the scope of these general instructions. All communications
issued by him relating to the census should be regarded as proceeding from the Government ; and as the time which
intervenes before the final enumeration is not more than sufficient for due preparation, all references on census matters
should be disposed_of with the utmost promptitude.

called a block ; these blocks will be grouped into circles, each of which will be under a supervisor ; and the circles, again,
will be grouped into charges, with a superintendent over each.
3. S i z e of sub-divisions.—The Census Committee appointed by the Government of India recommended that
each block should not contain more than 60 families or 300 souls ; and that a circle should not include more than 10
blocks, while the supervising agency should be strengthened for the final enumeration. At the last census each block
included 100 families (then called houses), and each circle 20 blocks in towns and 10 to 15 in scattered village areas.
In fixing these units, some discretion must be left to district officers, as the agency available and the distribution of the
people will vary greatly : but the limits for a block prescribed at the last census should not ordinarily be exceeded ;
while the supervisors should be appointed as nearly as possible on the scale recommended by the Committee. The size
of each block must be such that a single man can easily visit and correct the entries for every house in it in the course
of one night, and must be fixed with regard to the operations on the night of the census itself, and not to the work of
preliminary record. And it must be remembered that distance is an important element in the work of enumeration, and
that, where the houses lie close together and are easily accessible, the blocks may safely be larger than where opposite
conditions exist. Similar considerations will apply to the grouping of circles into charges, and four circles will probably
be found as much as a single superintendent can manage.
4.. B a s i s Of rural Sub-divisions.—The results of the sub-division into blocks, circles and charges will, when
complete, be shown in the enclosed Census Form A, which will be called the index-register, and must be prepared
separately for each tahsil. In no case must any block, circle or charge include lands situated in more than one tahsil.
The index-register will be based, in the first instance, upon the mauz<nvar (not mehit zedr) lists which you have in your
record offices. The sub-division must be one of area ; that is to say, it will not be sufficient merely to divide off the
towns and village sites ; but the land attached to them must also be divided off, so that every square yard of ground in
the district shall be included in some one enumerator's block. In the rural portions of the district, village boundaries will form the primary sub-divisions; and all outlying hamlets (dikhili tnazrali), and all squatters' huts of which
the occupants contemplate a stay of two or three months or longer, should be shown in column 6 opposite the name of
the village, subject, however, to the instructions contained in paragraph 7 below. This point becomes of special
importance where the villages really consist of a number of small scattered hamlets without any main homestead.
Where two villages have a common homestead, they must be bracketed together. When the index-register, excepting
columns 8, 9 and 10, is complete for any tahsil, it must be carefully checked, to ensure that every village and
town is entered in it. Uninhabited village areas must be included in appropriate blocks and entered in their proper
places.
5. Definition Of town.—As it is important to distinguish urban from rural population, all towns, cantonments,
and civil lines, will be entered separately from the villages in the lands of which they are situated. In the last census a
population of 5,000 or more was held to constitute a town ; and this rule will still be adhered to. Moreover, all municipalities will be classed as towns, whatever their population. It is, however, possible that there may be cases falling
under neither of these rules where the population is really urban rather than rural. Wherever the population does not
essentially consist merely of a village community, with its attendant menials and traders, but also includes a considerable
extraneous society drawn to the spot by the presence of a shrine of peculiar sanctity, by the existence of a centre of loc-1
commerce, or by other reasons, it should, if the whole population be of considerable magnitude, be treated as a town
District officers should therefore carefully consider all such cases, and report the names of all towns with a population
of less than 5,000, not being municipalities, and the grounds on which they propose to class them as such. It must, of
course, be remembered that a town must consist of a more or less coherent group of houses ; and that five separate and
district hamlets of 1,000 souls each, though they might be all included in a single village area, would not constitute
a town.
6. D e m a r c a t i o n of urban limits.—The limits of each town, cantonment and civil lines, must be carefully
defined. The area so marked off should include not only the ground actually occupied by buil lings, but also the waste
land immediately surrounding and attached to the town, and corresponding with the goirah de/i iu villages, and also all
land upon which people found residing on the night of the census would properly be included in the urban population ;
for instance, encamping-grounds. In the case of municipalities the municipal limits will ordinarily suffice. But there
may be suburbs which, though lying beyond these limits, really form, as regards their population, an integral portion of
the town; and such must be included with it for purposes of census. So, on the other hand, there may be cases, as at
Jullundur-Mahitpur, and at Urmar-Tanda in Hoshiarpur, where groups of houses bearing distinct names are included
within the same municipal limits ; and in such cases it will be necessary to consider whether these groups virtually constitute distinct towns, or whether the distinction is one of name only. If the latter is the case, the two should be treated
as one, their names being bracketed together. It will probably be possible to discover, from the records of the last
census, what limits were then adopted for each town; and if it becomes necessary, in consequence of the above instructions, to alter those limits in such a manner as materially to affect the comparison between the populations of iS6S and
1881, a report should be made specifying the reasons for, and the exact nature and extent of, the alteration. As regards
the boundaries of cantonments, there can be no difficulty; nor should the determination of the limits of civil lines be
difficult. Great care should be taken that the limits fixed in all cases are clearly defined on the spot by roads, or some
such unmistakable lines.

General Letter B, dated 15th September 1880.
From—DENZIL IBBETSON, Esq., Deputy Superintendent, Census Operations, Punjab,
To—All Commissioners and Deputy Commissioners in the Punjab.
In paragraph 8 of my General Letter A, dated 31st August 1880, I pointed out that the final demarcation of certain blocks could not be made till the house-lists were drawn up. I have now the honour to enclose instructions regarding the numbering of houses, and the preparation of the lists of houses and families upon which the actual enumeration
must be based. No pains should be spared to render these lists absolutely correct and complete, as they will form one
main check upon the completeness of the preliminary record.

2. Supplementary instructions to be issued by district officers.—There are several points upon which
supplementary instructions will be needed ; but, as these will refer only to special local peculiarities, or to large towns,
over all operations in which a much closer personal supervision will be both necessary and possible than in the case of
villages, I do not burden the general instructions with details which will be without meaning to a large majority of the
persons to whom they are directed, but leave local officers to issue such special orders as are necessary.
3. R u l e 4 : Definition of " h o u s e " a n d "family."—Ordinarily speaking, the outer door leading into the
stieet will be the sign of a house, which will include all the dwellings to which that door lead.-.. Each hotw: thus
denned will be distinguished by a separate number; and it is important that this numbering should be done very carefully, and on one uniform system, as the census will be by houses, and the instructions to enumerators will define a house
merely as a building or locality which bears a separate number.
In villages the definition will probably give no trouble; detached houses at wells or elsewhere will be numbered
separately; and the " road or street "will of course include blind-alleys upon which several separate dwelling-houses
open. But it is believed that, in some of the large cities, there are huge caravanserais, courts, or collections of quarters
entered by a common gateway, respecting which some doubt may be felt as to whether they should be treated as single
houses, or broken up into several houses. It is impossible to lay down profitably any general rule respecting these- ami
similar buildings; and each case must be dealt with on its merits. On the one hand, the house, as now defined, no
longer possesses any great statistical importance, and is, in fact, little more than a convenient unit to serve as the basi^
of enumeration. On the other hand, just because it is that unit, and because the enumerators will merclv be told that
a house is a building separately numbered, it is extremely important that each house so separately numbered and
recorded in the house-lists should be very clearly demarcated. Thus, where the definition given in the rule leaves it
open to doubt what should be taken to constitute a house, or where the letter of the definition would give an unmanageably large house, the following are the chief considerations by which you must be guided :—
(1) That the house should be clearly defined by obvious existing boundaries, so that there maybe no possibility
of mistake as to what it does or does not include.
(2) That it should not include an unw-ieldy number of families, as its doing so might lead to great complication in the actual enumeration. Thus, the ordinary sa-ai would be treated as a single house ; while where
a very large number of separate dwelling-houses - opened upon a single yard or court, they would be
numbered as separate houses, although the court might have a gateway common to them all, and leading
on to the road or street proper.
Doubtful cases of this sort must be carefully considered ; and supervisors of circles in which they are likely to occur
should be specially warned and instructed on the subject, and directed to apply for orders wherever they feel the least
doubt. The definition of a family should present no difficulty.
4. R u l e 5 : S k e t c h - m a p of d w e l l i n g - s i t e s . — T h e s e maps should be mere diagrams without any pretence to
accuracy, and merely showing the relative positions of the houses and the number of each. In some districts, maps of
village sites already exist, and will be of great assistance. But great care must be taken that they are not adopted as
they stand, but are merely used as the basis of new maps, which shall embody all changes that may have taken place
since the old maps were made.
5. R u l e 6 : H o u s e s t o b e n u m b e r e d . — T h i s is a most important rule, and should be carefully explained, and
strict adherence to its provisions enforced. The reason of the first part of the rule will be made apparent In a perus.il
of paragraphs 9 and 10 of General Letter A. The object of the latter part of the rule is, to ensure that every locality
included within the area of the block in which people are likely to be found on the night of the census, whether technically speaking it be a house or not, shall be included, first in the house-list, and then in the enumerator's schedules at
the preliminary record, so that there may be no danger of its being overlooked in the hurrv of the final enumeration.
The greatest care must be taken that the sketch-map does really include everything that, under this rule, it should
include, before further steps are taken. It will be productive of great confusion if houses have to be added after the
house-list is once made out and the house-numbers affixed.
6. R u l e 7 : F i n a l d e m a r c a t i o n of blocks.—This rule will only refer to blocks of which the exact limits have
been left undetermined under the circumstances described in paragraph 8 of Gener-al Letter A. You v ill have previously decided upon the number of such blocks to be formed in each village or mithalhi/i, and will have roughlv
indicated their boundaries ; and their final demarcation may ordinarily be left to the supervisors, with the aid and under
the control of their superintendents. But where there is likely to be difficulty, and generally in large towns, vim should
direct, that the proposed blocks be submitted to the principal officer in charge of the tahsil or town, for approval, before
the final numbering is begun, as it would be most inconvenient to have to alter the limits of blocks after the numbering
was completed.
7. R u l e 8 : S e r i a l n u m b e r i n g . — I t is possible that,'in some large municipalities, numbering by slivets has
already been effected, and in some districts the old census numbers still exist in the villages, cut on the doors of the
. houses. If this numbering agrees in principle with that now-prescribed —that is, if iis unit corresponds with (he house
as now defined—it may be adopted ; care being taken to add isolated houses and localities, such as are described in rule
5, at the end of the series. But some gazetted officer must satisfy himself by actual inspection that the agreement in
principle is real before sanction is given to the retention of the old numbering, remembering that what was called an
enclosure in 1868 is now called a house, and what was called a house is now called a family.
8. R u l e 9 : N u m b e r i n g of h o u s e s . — I n some districts the numbers at the last census were cut by the village
carpenter on the lintels Of the entrance-doors of the houses. This seems a good plan where it can be arranged for. Of
course, it cannot be followed for the numbers of temporary houses. The method of marking the numbers is left open
in the rule, and district officers must issue orders on the subject.

General Letter C, dated 23rd September 1880.
From—DF.NZIL IBBF.TSON, Esq., Deputy Superintendent, Census Operations, Punjab,
To—All Commissioners and Deputy Commissioners in the Punjab.
I have the honour to enclose copies of instructions for the guidance of enumerators, and also of instructions to
supervisors regarding the preliminary record. I do not think it necessary to frame separate instructions for superintendents; but they must explain the rules to the supervisors, and put them through a preliminary course of filling up
practice-schedules, just as supervisors are directed to do with their enumerators in rule 17 of their instructions. The
officer in charge of each tahsil or town must satisfy himself that every superintendent and supervisor thoroughly understands the rules before he is allowed to begin the instruction of the enumerators.
2. You will notice that paragraph 17 of the supervisors' instructions does not contemplate the existence of any enumerators who will not make their own preliminary record. If there are any such (see paragraph 11 of my General
Letter A), it should be explained that this rule-does not apply to these men.
3. Blank enumerators' schedules will be supplied to you, together with vernacular instructions and schedules
filled up by way of example. These will have to be stitched up into books, one for each block according to the
requirements of each. Rough bindings will be supplied to you, together with the forms, and a few sample books will also
accompany them.
4. The only columns the entries in which present the least difficulty are those for religion, caste and occupation.
Our object is to collect information in these columns in the greatest possible detail. So little is really known about the
religious, tribal and industrial divisions of the people, that no selection is possible in the first instance. Thus the entries
in the schedules cannot be in too minute detail in these respects. It will be easy to reject what is unimportant when we
come to tabulate, but it will then be most difficult to obtain details which may not hove been recorded in the first
instance. The instructions on the subject of religion and occupation are sufficiently clear; and it will only be necessarv
to see that they are carried out exactly.
5. It is perhaps impossible to frame instructions regarding the three sub-columns of column 7 which shall apply
exactly to all cases. It is hoped that the numerous examples given in the sample schedule ' will help to explain what
is wanted, but it is probable that further directions will be needed in some districts regarding the mode of entrv of
specific tribes. While care must be taken that the great caste or tribe, as a whole, to which each person belongs, and
not merely his local clan, is shown in the first sub-column, it is by no means intended that mere theories or traditions of
tribal origin should be acted upon. A Titvana or a Sid! is actually a Rajput, and should be entered as such ; but a
Khatri, whatever be.the origin of the tribe, is not recognised as a Rajput, and should be shown as Khatri. So, again,
an Arora should be entered as such, and not as a Khatri. It is believed that in the Western Punjab the word Jat is often
used to include almost all Mahomedan agriculturists of Indian origin who are not Rajputs, and that the name Gujar is
similarly applied to shepherds, of whatever caste. The term Hindki, too, is often used in a very loose and indefinite way,
as if it were the name of a separate caste. Care must be taken that these words are not carelessly used in their general
sense, but that the real caste of each person is asked for and entered. It should be noted that the clans meant to be
entered in the second sub-column are locally more extensive than, and include several of. the gois or septs which are to
be entered in the third sub-column : no sub-division lower than the got is needed. It may be necessarv to guard against
a tendency of the enumerators to fill up all three sub-columns, whether the corresponding sections of the caste exist or
not. The second sub-column will probably be empty for most menial castes ; and for Jats. Giijars and Rajputs in most
of the Eastern Punjab.
6. It is during the preparation and subsequent scrutiny of the preliminary record that personal supervision on the
part of superior officers will be most essential. During this time the whole of the district staff should be incessantly
moving about from village to village, explaining, correcting and supervising.

2. P r e p a r a t i o n Of h o u s e - l i s t s . — T h e first thing to be done is to prepare a house-list of all buildings within

It is necessary, I find, to issue explicit instructions for the guidance of officers in classifying, as is proposed in Form 1
and elsewhere, the population of villages and towns.
2. The difficulty to be met is that of determining what constitutes a town as distinguished from a village.
3. The rule hitherto has been this : Every separately named locality having 5,000 or more inhabitants has. for tabulation purposes in reporting the census results of former years, been treated as a town. With a population below 5,000
the locality has been considered a village.
4. This has been so elastic a system that, in the reports dealing with the census figures, places have been treated as
towns which are nothing more than a collection of separate villages—in some instances bearing an identical name, in
others not even possessing this pretext for such treatment.
5. It has also happened that, under existing local laws, either for municipal or for police purposes, villages have been
grouped together into unions, and in those cases where the population has amounted to or has exceeded 5,000, the
locality has been entered as a town in the census records.
6. It is essential that such mistakes shall not be repeated on the present occasion.
7. I request, therefore, you will ascertain the views of the Government in this matter, and that you will let me know
whether the following suggestions will provide for such difficulties as may possibly arise.
8. It will probably be necessary to define, with some attempt at precision, what is a town, in contra-distinction to a
village. It seems to me the accompanying definition would meet all requirements :
" W h e r e the population is not less than 5,000, and resides in one assemblage of houses, the houses being- either
in juxtaposition or only separated by streets, or by spaces surrounded on all sides by houses, or by gardens or spaces,
whether cultivated or not, distinctly appertaining to the houses in question, such assemblage of houses, if bearing one
common name, shall be considered a town."
g. This definition, however, if accepted, will not take in all those cases where the population may fairly be considered urban; on the other hand, it will embrace cases where, though the population is not less than 5,000, the place is a
village, and the inhabitants purely agricultural.
10. I know of cases where an ambitious dependant, or high officer of a former Mogul Emperor, has built a small
walled town on land belonging to an agricultural village, and has settled traders in it. The lapse of years, perhaps
centuries, has found this a flourishing centre of trade, the population purely urban, but not numbering 5,000 inhabitants.
though but little short of that figure. I know, too, of cases where a population purely agricultural has been included in
the town population of a province (the inhabitants of the village being more in number than 5,000).
1 1 . It may be thought desirable to treat such cases specially, excluding the purely agricultural population from the
town, and including the purely town population under its appropriate heading, without regard in either case to the number
of inhabitants. If there are such cases, they must be well known to district officers, and, on .your representing them, and
giving reasons for treating them exceptionally, I shall be willing to deal with these instances as may appear most appropriate.

was no municipality. The village tables in the Punjab will be based upon the mauzawir lists of the revenue office ; and
this will in most cases render impossible such an error as is alluded to in paragraph 4 of your Circular. In the hilly
tracts, however, the mauzah often includes a large number of separate petty hamlets; while it is just possible that some of
the villages of the Western Punjab, which often consist of mere congeries of wells, may attain the limits of 5,000 souls.
To guard against these cases special precautions will be necessary.
6. The Lieutenant-Governor is also of opinion, with reference to paragraphs 9 and 10 of your Circular, that every
conterminous collection of houses which contains 5,000 souls should be treated as a town without further inquiry as to
the nature of its population. Places of this importance, even when held by a cultivating community, always form centres
of local trade, and include a very considerable urban population. It may be said generally that it is often difficult to
decide in respect of any given place whether its population is rather urban or rural in its composition ; and an arbitrary
rale of the sort proposed seems both necessary and unobjectionable.

5. Definition of town.—As it is important to distinguish urban from rural population, all towns, cantonments
and civil lines will be entered separately from the villages in the lands of which they are situated. In the last census, a
population of 5,000 or more was held to constitute a town ; and this rule will still be adhered to. Moreover, all municipalities will be classed as towns, whatever their population. It is, however, possible that there may be cases falling
under neither of these rules, where the population is really urban rather than rural. Wherever the population does not
essentially consist merely of a village community, with its attendant menials and traders, but also includes a considerable extraneous society drawn to the spot by the presence of a shrine of peculiar sanctity, by the existence of a centre of
local commerce, or by other reasons, it should, if the whole population be of considerable magnitude, be treated as a
town. District Officers should therefore carefully consider all such cases, and report the names of all towns with a
population of less than 5,000, not being municipalities, and the grounds on which they propose to class them as such.
It must, of course, be remembered that a town must consist of a more or less coherent group of houses; and that five
separate and distinct hamlets of 1,000 souls each, though they might be all included in a single village area, would
not constitute a town.
6. D e m a r c a t i o n of urban limits.—The limits of each town, cantonment and civil lines must be carefully
defined. The area so marked off should include not only the ground actually occupied by buildings, but also the
waste land immediately surrounding and attached to the town, and corresponding to the goira dch in villages, and also
all land upon which people found residing on the night of the census would properly be included in the urban population ; for instance, encamping-grounds. In the case of municipalities, the municipal limits will ordinarily suffice. But
there may be suburbs which, though lying beyond these limits, really form, as regards their population, an integral portion
of .the town ; and such must be included with it for purposes of census. So, on the other hand, there may be cases, as
at Jullundur-Mahitpur, and at Urmar-Thanda in Hoshiarpur, where groups of houses bearing distinct names are included
within the same municipal limits; and in such cases it will be necessary to consider whether these groups virtually constitute distinct towns, or whether the distinction is one of name only. If the latter is the case, the two should be treated
as one, their names being bracketed together. It will probably be possible to discover, from the records of last census, what
limits were then adopted for each town; and if it becomes necessary, in consequence of the above instructions, to alter
those limits in such a manner as materially to affect the comparison between the populations of 1868 and 1SS1, a report
should be made specifying the reasons for, and the exact nature and extent of, the alteration. As regards the boundaries
of cantonments, there can be no difficulty; nor should the determination of the limits of civil lines be difficult. Great
care should be taken that the limits fixed in all cases are clearly defined on the spot by roads, or some such unmistakeable lines.
