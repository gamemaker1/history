Foreword
APART ftom laying the foundations of demography in this subcontinent, a hundred years of the
Indian Census has also produced 'elaborate and
scholarly accounts of the variegated phenomen of
Indian life s~metimes with no statistics attached,
but usually with just enough statistics to give
empirical under-pinning to their conclusions'. In a
country, largely illiterate, where statistical or
numerical comprehension of even such aJ simple
thing as age was liable to be inaccurate, an
understanding of t,pe social structure was essential.
It was more nec~sary to attain a broad understanding of what w?-s happening around oneself
than to wrap oneSelf up in 'statistical ingenuity'
or 'mathematical tVanipulation'. This explains
why the Indian Census came to be interested in
'many by-paths' ami 'nearly every branch of
scholarship, from adthropology and sociology to
geography and religion'.
In the last few decades the Census has increasingly
turned its efforts to the. presentation of village
statistics. This suits ,the teqlper of the times as
well as our political :and economic structure.
Fo~ even as we have a great deal of, centralization
,on the one hand and qecentralization on the other,
my colleagues thought it would be a welcome continuation of the Census tradition to try to invest
the dry bones of vjllage statistics with flesh-andblood accounts of social structure and social change.
It was accordingly decided to select a few villages
in every State for special study, where personal
observation would be brought to bear on the interpretation of statistics to find out how much of a
village was static and yet changing and how fast
the winds of change were ~lowing and from where.
Randomness of selection was, therefore, eschewed. There was no intention to build up a picture
for the whole State in quantitative terms on the
basis of villages selected statistically at random.
The selection was avowedly purposive: the object
being as much to find out what was happening
and how fast to those villages which had fewer
reasons to choose change and more to remain
lodged in the past as to discover how the more
'normal' types of villages were changing. They
were to be primarily type studies which, by virtue
of their number and distribution, would also

give the reader a 'feel' of what was going on and
some kind of a map of the country.
\ A brief account of the tests of selection will help
'to explain. A minimum of thirty-five villages
was to be chosen with great care to :represent
adequately geographical, occupational and even
ethnic diversity. Of this minimum of thirty-five,
the distribution was to be as follows:
(a) At least eight villages were to be so selected
that each of them would contain one dominant
community with one predominating occupation,
e.g., fishermen, forest workers, jhum festivals and
fairs. There were to be recordings, of course,
of cultural and social traits and occupational
mobility. This was followed up in March 1960
by two specimen schedules, one for each household,
the other for the village as a whole, which, apart
from spelling out the mode of inquiry suggested
in the September 1959 conference, introduced
groups of questions aimed at sensing changes in
attitude and behaviour in such fields as marriage,
inheritance, moveable and immoveable property
cultivators, potters, weavers, salt-makers, quarry
workers etc. A village should have a minimum
population of 400, the optimum being between
500 and 700.
(b) At least seven villages were to be of numerically prominent scheduled Tribes of the State.
Each village could represent a particular tribe.
The minimum population should be 400, the
optimum being between 500 and 700.
(c) The third group of villages should each
be of fair size, of an old and settled character and
contain variegated occupations and be, if possible,
multi-ethnic in composition. By fair size was
meant a population of 500-700 persons or more.
The village should mainly depend on agriculture
and be sufficiently away from the major sources
modern communication such as the district administrative headquarters and business centres. It should
be roughly a day's journey from the above places.
The villages were to be selected with an eye to variation in terms of size, proximity to city and other
means of modern communication, nearness to hills,
jungles and major rivers. Thus there was to be
,a regional distribution throughout the State of this

It is a unique feature of these village surveys that

above the crushing load of the 1961 Census.
It might be of interest to recount briefly the stages
by which the Survey enlarged its scope. At the

Survey set itself the task of what might be called
a record of material traits, like settlement
patterns of the village; house types; diet; dress;
ornaP1ents and foot-wear; furniture and storing
vessels; common means of transport of goods
and passengers; demestication of animals and
birds; markets attended ; worship of deities;
plainly the intention to provide adequate statistical
support to empirical 'feel', to approach, qualitative
change through statistical quantities. It had been
difficult to give thought to the importance of 'just
enough statistics to give empirical underpinning
to conclusion', at a time when my colleagues were
straining themselves to the utmost for the succesS
of the main Census operations, but once the census
count itself was left behind in March, 1961, a series
of three regional seminars in Trivandrum (May
1961), Darjeeling and Srinagar (June 1961) restored
their attention to this field and the importance

RAJANA lies 9 miles away from Dadahu and it IS
one of the big villages. around here at 5,500 feet
in trans-Git;i area of Sirmur District. The village
lies on a hi\! slope. The upper portion is known

After crossing the logar stream, there is a four

The predominant castes residing in Rajana are:
Brahmins, Kolis and Rajputs. Other castes are

History of the Village
According to revenue records "About five
hundred years ago, one Puja came from Delhi
and settled at the present site of the village with
permission of the ruler of Sirmur State. He did
not pay for the land. At that time, the site was
uninhabited. There was a dense forest all roundA pine tree grew near Plija's house. The village
was first known as KaiIar. This name was derived
from the pine tree-Kail".
"After two hundred years, two men named Kadwa
and Dharwa, came from Palar village and requested Puja for some land. These Bhats were given
the land. Since then the Brahmin population
continued to increase. They gradually owned
some land".

During those days, Maharaja Mahi Prakash
Bahadur of Sirmur State changed the name of the
lillage from Kailar to Rajana. The village grew
large by the passage of time. There is no fort in
th e vicinity".
Upper Rajana is known as Kunder. The lower
portion is called Gadh.
According to a legend narrated by Ujagar
Singh, a few hundred years ago, the Raja of
Sirmur was visiting the ruler of lubbal State.
Some misunderstandings suddenly cropped up
between the two rulers and matters worsened.
Raja of Sirmur had to flee for his life from lubbal.
During this journey, the Raja had to take refuge
in the house of a Brahmin in the village. Since
then the village came to be known as Rajana
which appears to be derived from Raja-ana or
the coming of a Raja.
Tomar

The. Rajputs were the first settlers who came from
Delhi. Bishna Lambardar has narrated that orginally
tHey were three brothers. Two of them settled in
lower Rajana and the third on a nearby spur of a
hill. In the initial stages, they gradually got a grip
of the place and extended their influences over the
surrounding area and off and ·on quarrelled with
local people and' finally conquered the area
comprising fifty-two villages to which village
patwari testifies to the version by saying that
the Rajputs of Rajana own landed property in fiftytwo surrounding villages. The first descendents
of these Rajputs also settled in Kamrao and
Dagaihada villages of Paonta and Nahan tehsils.
Another version about the history of the village
is that about five hundred years ago, Kanauj was
ruled by a Raja of Tomar dynasty and in a nearby
state, ruled his two nephews named; Prithviraj
and laichand. These states were at constant
war with Sultans of Delhi and this fued continued
for a long period. During Aurangzeb's reign,
some of the families from Tomar dynasty had to
flee this area via Punjab. In Punjab they spent
some time but could not live peacefully in the face
of subsequent repeated Mohammadan invasions
from the west. When they arrived in Rajana,
Tomars found families of Rajputs and Brahmins

Physical Aspects

Rajana nestles over a gentle slope of a hill,
Surat-ka-Tibba at 77°. 25' E and 30°. 14' N. The
other peaks surrounding the village are Kamliwala-tibba and Iamdagni-wala-tibba which are
branches of the Shiwalik hills. 12,000 feet high
Chur peak known as Chur Chandni the mountain
of the silver bangle, snowbound for most of the year,
can be seen towards the south, the summit of Suratka-tibba.
A stream Barag-ka-khala, has derived its name
from village Barag, three miles away. This flows
about a mile away and gets flooded during rains.

The area surrounding the village is dry, inhospitable and poor in vegetation. The soil is thin
and stoney and not very fertile.

Drinking water

Kuhl and tank

In lower Rajana villagers fetch water from a
spring which a is furlong away. Cool and sweet
water gushes out from this spring known as tawana.
There is more water during the rains. The spring is
surrounded by a stone wall with a raised platform
in the middle.

A small irrigation channel has been drawn from
tawana spring which runs a zig-zag way along
Dadahu-Rajana path and after passing through
lower Rajana terminates at the other end. Water
is ayailable throughout the year for irrigating the
fields lying below the path.

In upper Rajana, two springs are located at one
end. One is used by the swaran castes while the
other by the Harijans. The water in the spring is
little and in summer it has to be fetched from
tawana. During the days of scarcity, villagers form
a queue by arranging their brass pitchers in a
row. The water from tawana is carried· during
night when the peasants have no work and the
weather is pleasant.

Two more small irrigation channels have been
taken out from another spring a mile away towards
Dadahu which irrigates fields between Maina and
Rajana.
One irrigation tank is under construction in lower
Rajana, when this is compieted surplus water will
be stored. The tank is being constructed near the

Communications
During heavy rains, the path between Dadahu
and Rajana remains in a bad condition. It is sometimes repaired by villagers of nearby villages, or by
the rich shopkeepers of Dadahu. The wooden
crossing over the Giri is washed away when the
river is in spate. This is repaired afterwards. The
shopkeepers undertake the repair work of foot
paths and the bridge to control trade in ginger.
Post office

One branch post office opened in February, 1957
is run by a post master and a postman. This
post office serves fourteen villages within five miles.
Prior to this, dak used to be distributed once a
week by a postman from Dadahu. Now dak is
delivered on alternate days.

Trade
Ginger is the chief agricultural produce exported
from here. Farmers carry ginger down to Dadahu
in conical baskets or kiftas on their backs and sell
it to the sllOpkeepers. From Dadahu, it is lifted
in trucks and sold at Nahan, Ambala, Amritsat
and Delhi. Some of the shopkeepers send salesmen to Ra'jana from Dadahu where they purchase
the ginger at the rates prevailing at Dadahu. Thus,
farmers are saved from the· trouble and time
involved in carrying their ginger to Dadahu. In
return, they buy cotton cloth, metal-ware and
other utensils, salt, cigarettes, jaggery and kerosene.
Villagers also purchase goods from a local
shopkeeper of Maina village and from the two
shopkeepers of Rajana.

Patinday-This is the most liked form of bread.

siddu-These are known as roto. Thick breads

Askali-Is another preparation which is made
from wheat, maize and koda flour. This is made
with the flat stone having some round depressions
called sacha. Kneaded rice 'flour is put into the

Suchavday-This type of food is like sewian prepared in plains. The method of cooking is different.
It is made of wheat flour or of mandawa. Flour

Biloi-These are known as kanjan. Tehse are

Atta-Some wheat is mixed with boiling syrup ..

Muda-lt is made of wheat, maize or rice. Wheat
and maize are parched. In the case of rice, some
paddy is first soaked and then parched. It is then

with jaggery or illachidana.
Gulli-Meat is known as gulti and villagers
are very fond of meat. Goat's meat is common.
Beaf is absolutely prohibited. It is customary
for every household to slaughter at least one goat
in the month of Magha, when relatives visit them.
These slaughtered goats are hung with a rope in the
upper storey of the house. This dries up and
is relished throughout the winter months. Fish
known as moshhi is also consumed by the geo9te
after catching it from Giri, Jogar streams and
Barag-ka-khada.
There are other dishes like, halwa, khir and

anyone eating eggs. But younger people like

is liked more than sugar. Whenever a villager
visits Dadahu for selling. his ginger, he brings
some sweets like [addo, baisan-ki-barfi and pakoda.
Fruits like pears, plums, peaches and apricots
are taken. whenever they are available. Tea is
taken by almost all the households. Some better
off families have tea daily while others only during
the winter. Tea made with jaggery is liked very
much. Pure desi ghee is used in the daily diet by
the people. Vanaspati ghee is also used.
But on special occasions like marriages or religious
katha, pure des; ghee is lavishly used. Infants
and children are provided with milk but a major
portion of it is converted into curds and ghee.
Smoking-Men and women enjoy a puff on the
hukka. Old people confine themselves to smoking
tobaccoo. The younger generation is fond of
cigaJ;ettes. Even small school g~ing children smoke.
Wine-In local dialect wine is called sur. A few
people are in the habit of drinking wine. It is
distilled by fermenting jaggery or barley or manuwad.
Fuel ~nd Lighting

Rajana is not electrified. Tiny kerosene lamps
are used. A few families have lanterns. A bottle
of kerosene oil costs about fifty paisa.

Animal husbandry
Cattle are an indispensable asset to the villagers.
Bulls, buffaloes, cows, and goats reared by all castes
are of hill breed and are small. Average milk
yield of a cow is two to three seers in a day and that
of she buffaloes is 6 to 8 seers per day. They also
rear up goats and sheep. Shearing is done twice
in a year; once in the month of Bhadra ·and the
second time in the month of Phalguna. The
wool is spun, and woven by weavers to make
garments. When a goat dies by accident or due
to some illness, its meat is consumed by the family,
and a bag is made out of its skin. To make a
bag the skin is rubb~d with cow-dung and filled
with mandowa grain. Ash or wheat straw may be

substituted for mandowa. This is kept filled up
for about one week, the contents are taken out
and the skin is dried in the sun and a bag commonly known as khaltto is made.
A bull is yoked at the age of four years and cows
begin to give milk at the same age or a little
later.
When an animal dies, the carcase is removed
by the cobbler. The skin is removed tanned and
shoes made. These shoes are known as joday.
The animals are set free in the morning along
with a member of household and are driven home
in the evening. This person may be a young boy
or a small girl. If an elder goes along some spinning
may be done in the forest.
Animals are given dry hay, green leaves, residue
of the flour after sieving and some dry bread
left from the previous night. They are given
salt at intervals._ Dry hay, is cut from pasture in
December and January and stored for the remaining
period. The grass is stacked in an open ridge
or slopes near the village in high conicals stacks.
Cattle are kept in dochie where a member of the
household looks after them. The physical condition of the cattle is poor due to lack of nourishing
fodder. The average life of a buffaloe is fifteen
years while a cow or a bull may live upto 13 years.·
Poultry is becoming popular, young men like
to eat eggs. The Gram Sewak has distributed
some improved varieties of poultry birds among a
few farmers.

Villagers give local treatment to cattle in the
village because it becomes difficult to take a sick
animal to Dadahu although Dadahu is not so far
away. It is very rare that a cattle is taken to the

by the veterinary stockman in the village. The

(1911-1933) that villagers did not have any
rights in other adjoining forest because, they
had their own shamlat. Now on the lines of that

is available at market rates. However, they
were entitled for free timber from their own
shamlat deh, for which application was to be
forwarded to Divisional Forest Officer through
patwari halka. If any person was found cutting
tree in shamlat deh without prior approval, the
case was referred to the Deputy -Commissioner.
For obtaining timber from Government forest
the application is forwarded through the Panchayat. If any person is found cutting 'trees without approval, he is fined.

INHERITANCE OF PROPERTY

Before the enforcement of Hindu law of succession Act, sons inherited the property and
daughters were debarred from inheriting their
father's property. Generally property was equally divided among the sons. In case of some,
disputes among the sons, the matter was settled
by elders of the village. The wish of the dying
father was respected.
In families having polyandry the division takes
place on the basis of the number of brothers.
All sons have a claim' to inherit their father's
property.
A widow may inherit her husband's property
when she does not marry the second' time. On
getting married again, she looses her claims upon
the property which passes to her husband's
brother or even to other relatives.

Religion

Local deities of Braharup and Shrigul are
worshipped on festivals like Lohri and Meghi-kaSaja. Rainka Mata is held in higher reverence than
other gods special worships are held for her when
one is in troubles and sometimes to achieve one's
ends. Religious books like Ramayana and
Mahabharata influence the people. They worship
gods like Shiva on Shivratri and Laxmi and Rama
on Dewali festivals. Hindu customs are followed
on marriage and death. 'The fatalistic view of
life karma and Puraney Janam-ke-Sanskar are
common belief here. Many villagers keep fasts
on Puranmashi, Janamashtmi and Shivratri.
Basti Ram Rajput living in upper Rajana
is a staunch follower of Arya Smaj. He is a
vegetarian and does not like goat slaughter on
Maghi festival.

Common habits
. The people living in Transgiri have certain
peculiar common habits which require a brief
mention.
Smoking in the village is quite customary. Every
grown up boy or girl smokes. Men and women
do not feel shy smoking before their parents or inlaws. Among youngmen, drinking is common.
Men carry loads on their backs while women
carry loads on their heads. They joke if a women
carries load on her back and a man on his head.
All little and unmarried girls wear chak on their
heads. A man from the Transgiri has his loiyacoat on his shoulders.
Whenever a son is born, presents are given to the
mother. The child's maternal uncle or the grand
father visits her house with some presents. These
may be clothes and grains, including ghee and
jaggery. A villager must carry a small earthern
pot full of ghee whenever he visits his relatives.
Women do not feel shy in talking to a stranger
or the father-ilt-law or any other male member
of the family.
As a mark of respect, Scheduled Castes pass
by the left side of a Swaran Caste.
Men generally put on loin cloth- and not a
pyjama. Even before a large gathering they dance
with a shirt and a lion cloth on.

Panchayats
Before the- establishment of statutory Panchayat,
the study of Rajana shows that the vi11ag~ had its
own Panchayat. The village disputes on land,
cattle, theft or on social functions, were settled
by elders of Rajana. To start with, the dispute
was first reported to the village Lumberdar. He
used to call a meeting of five or six elderly people.
A compromise solution was found. Sometimes
the defaulter was fined Rs. 24 or a goat was slain
in honour of the judgement. In case the villager
refused to honour the judgement, the second
party was at liberty to take the issue to court.
Both parts of Rajana had separate Panchayats.
In case the parties at dispute belonged to these
parts four panches from each part formed the
panchayat which used to decide the dispute. To
head the Panchayat, an elderly person who commanded respect was selected. He was known as the
thagda.
Gram Panchayat.-In the past there were no
regular Panchayats. The Panchayat which covers
Rajana is known as Gram Panchayat, Rajana.
This came into existence on 26th June, 1954, with
headquarters at Maina. It covers eleven villages.
There are fifteen members. They are known as
panches. Eight seats are covered by Swaran and
seven are reserved for Harijans. A seat each from
Swaran and Harijan is reserved for women. From
Rajana, there are 11 panches in the Panchayat,
four Swaran and seven Scheduled Castes, two of
them are women.
There are separate constituencies for Swarans
and Harijans. The last elections -to the Gram Panchayat were held in 1962. In the first date, the
election had to be postponed, because the voters
were less than 125 of 1/6 or the total number
of voters required for the election. The second
date had to be fixed. On this date 209 voters
exercised their right. Casting of votes was by
raising hands. For the president, there were two
contestants, one Swaran and another Harijan.
The Swaran won. The election of the vice president
was nanimous.

Abandonment of Sita

heard some contradiction about Sita and himself.

day Rama was strolling at night in disguise. He

public. There is every likelihood of losing fame
of an ideal life. Therefore I am going to abandon

BUDHA-A SIRMUR FOLK DANCE

Budha is an interesting pance from Rapika tehsil.
This owes its origin to the bygone days when the
higher castes used to dominate the low castes, their
tenants, in the part of the hills. In this dance at the
time of Diwali festival, Thakurs and Rajputs send
their greetings to their relatives in another village
through the pancing party consisting of harijans.
Tl:e dancers ,also get a chance to wish a happy
Diwali to their relatives and friends while their arriva! is eagerly "waited in the village.
The tenants used to please their landlords in
many ways. One of them was by performing
Budha dance in 'their honour. This dance has
become a part of their cultural life for the last so
many decades andjs continuing even now, though
the time has changed. It is' performed during
Rainka fair which \is held about ten days after
Diwali. On various cultural meets at State and'
at the Natioljlal leVel, this dance has been performed and is highl~ accl~imed.
Budha dance is held on ~ej and chauth following
Diwali festival in the month of Kartika. It may,
however, be prolon~ed for a: day or so. The
minimum number of men ta)cing part in the dance
is ten while the maximum may be fifty. The entire
party is divided into two groups, one performs
the dance and the other plays the music. Songs
are sung in chorus by both.
The men in the dancing party wear a special
type of a dress. The other party wears the usual
clothes. The special dress consists of pagreea white tu,rban, kurta-shirt, pyjama-which is
tight at the ancles and a cholna- a white gown
extending upto the knee having fine embroidery
work with coloured threads done at the back and
on the sides. Round the waist a kamar dawal
is wrapped. This is a big coloured piece of cloth.
The members of the dancing party wear ornaments mostly borrowed from' their landlords.
These are dharaitoo and baley-ear-rjp.gs having
coloured stones, har- a heavy silver necklace,
kandi-a small silver necklace worn quite close
to the neck and battey-heavy and thick silver
bracelets.
The orchestra consists of haluk-a longish type
of small drum, damanu-a small nagara, chhunku-

a brass plate, bansh/i-aflute and dhak-a small
type of haluk.
On the appointeq day, Kolis from each household
collect in the open compound of the village
temple. They carry their musical instruments.
The special dress is, however, not put on at this
stage. A prayer known as sew a is first sung
before the village deity. After this they break 'into
two or three groups and go to the houses of their
landlords. They sing songs in their praise. This
too is known as sewa. After singing for about
two houses, they seek permission. The landlord
then asks them to visit the particular village where
he has his own daughter or sister married. The
dancing party then begins its journey to that
village, reaching there before dawn.
Before entering the village boundary, mUSIC IS
played. First, they call at the village deity and
after singing the sewa, they move towards the
particular house as directed by their landlord.
Here they sing sewa in honour of the family for
one hour and then retire for a short rest.
After a brief pause, the budha dance begins.
The eader of the party directs others to put on th~
dress and jewellery as described above. The
other dance to the tune of the music. The dance
is held in a circle which goes on moving slowly.
They wave a handkerchief or a dangra from time
time to time. The steps are not subject to any
rules. The tempo of the dance goes on increasing
slowly with the music. The dance continues till
in 'the evening. After interval, they go to the
village deity and again sing the sewa.
The family serves the party with delicious
food like rice, potanda, ghee and jaggery. The
party distributes -parched grain or muda, to .the
married daughters of the village. In return they
receive double the muda and grains. The landlord
gives them one rupee each. At night, they sing
and visit other houses:
Next .morning, the party returns to their village.
On theIr way back, they first visit their village
deity and sing sewa. Then they go the landlord's
house and sing songs. He receives muda and
walnuts at the hands of the members of the dancing
party. In return, Thakurs serve them with good
dishes and give them one rupee each. Thus the
